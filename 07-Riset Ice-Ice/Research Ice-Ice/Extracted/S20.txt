                  The Mekong Delta is one of the most productive agricultural regions in Southeast Asia
                  and plays a vital role in ensuring national food security while contributing significantly
                  to global rice and aquaculture markets [1]. However, the region is increasingly vulnerable
                  to the impacts of climate change, including sea-level rise, salinity intrusion, land subsid-
                  ence, and more frequent extreme weather events, all of which have altered hydrologi-
                  cal regimes and undermined ecosystem stability [2–4]. Anthropogenic land subsidence,
                  driven largely by intensive groundwater extraction, further compounds these pressures,
                  with annual subsidence rates reaching several centimetres in some locations and thereby
                  increasing relative sea-level rise, inundation, and salinization risks across the low-lying
                  Delta [5]. These changes are placing growing pressure on conventional agricultural sys-
                  tems, particularly in low-lying coastal areas where water scarcity and salinity intrusion
                  have become more severe and less predictable [6].
                    Traditional production systems in the Mekong Delta, especially intensive rice culti-
                  vation and shrimp monoculture, are becoming increasingly constrained under chang-
                  ing climatic and hydrological conditions [7, 8]. Rice farming, which depends heavily on
                  freshwater availability, is becoming less viable due to prolonged drought and saline water
                  intrusion [6, 9]. During normal dry seasons, water with salinity above 4 g L⁻1 typically
                  penetrates approximately 40–60 km inland, further increasing the vulnerability of fresh-
                  water-dependent rice production [10]. At the same time, shrimp monoculture, although
                  economically attractive in the short term, has often been associated with environmental
                  degradation, including mangrove loss, water pollution, and greater disease risks [11, 12].
                  Together, these pressures highlight the urgent need for alternative production models
                  that are both environmentally sustainable and economically resilient [8, 13].
                    In this context, seaweed aquaculture has emerged as a promising nature-based solu-
                  tion that aligns with the goals of sustainable development and climate adaptation [14].
                  Seaweed farming provides multiple ecological and socioeconomic co-benefits, including
                  carbon uptake, nutrient removal from eutrophic waters, and coastal protection through
                  wave attenuation [15]. It also offers opportunities for income diversification and liveli-
                  hood resilience among coastal communities, particularly in areas where the productiv-
                  ity of traditional agriculture and aquaculture is declining [16]. In addition, integrating
                  seaweed into existing aquaculture systems, such as shrimp ponds, may improve resource
                  efficiency and reduce environmental externalities [17].
                    Despite growing interest in seaweed aquaculture, existing studies on this sector in
                  Vietnam and the broader region remain fragmented [18, 19]. Previous research has
                  tended to examine ecological functions, market and value-chain issues, or governance
                  and policy dimensions separately, rather than through an integrated lens [18, 19]. As
                  a result, comprehensive analyses that connect these dimensions to assess the develop-
                  ment potential of seaweed aquaculture in the Mekong Delta are still limited. This gap
                  limits the evidence base needed to design coordinated strategies for the sustainable scal-
                  ing of the sector under climate change [19]. Addressing this gap requires an integrated
                  synthesis that connects environmental, economic, and institutional evidence within
                  the specific context of the Mekong Delta. Accordingly, this review synthesizes current
                  knowledge on seaweed aquaculture development in the Mekong Delta by integrating

                  2 Methodological approach
                  This study adopted a structured narrative review approach to synthesize current knowl-
                  edge on the development of seaweed aquaculture in the Mekong Delta under climate
                  change. The review was not designed as a systematic review and was not preregistered.
                  Accordingly, PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-
                  Analyses) 2020 items were considered only where applicable for reporting transparency,
                  without implying full PRISMA compliance. The procedures for literature identification,
                  screening, eligibility assessment, inclusion, and thematic synthesis were explicitly docu-
                  mented. The review covered the environmental, economic, technological, market, social,
                  and institutional dimensions of seaweed aquaculture, with particular emphasis on evi-
                  dence relevant to climate adaptation and sustainable coastal development in the Mekong
                  Delta.
                    Initial literature searches were conducted in January 2025 and updated on 30 June 2026
                  using Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar. Additional policy and technical doc-
                  uments were identified through searches of the official repositories and websites of the
                  Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations, the World Bank, the World
                  Wide Fund for Nature, the United Nations Conference on Trade and Development, and
                  relevant Vietnamese institutions. The searches combined terms related to the produc-
                  tion system, geographical scope, and analytical themes. In Scopus, the exact search syn-
                  tax was: TITLE-ABS-KEY(("seaweed aquaculture" OR "seaweed farming" OR "seaweed
                  cultivation") AND ("Mekong Delta" OR Vietnam) AND ("climate change" OR adaptation
                  OR "ecosystem services" OR livelihood* OR "value chain" OR policy OR governance)). In
                  Web of Science Core Collection, the corresponding syntax was: TS=(("seaweed aquacul-
                  ture" OR "seaweed farming" OR "seaweed cultivation") AND ("Mekong Delta" OR Viet-
                  nam) AND ("climate change" OR adaptation OR "ecosystem services" OR livelihood* OR
                  "value chain" OR policy OR governance)). In Google Scholar, where field tags and trun-
                  cation functions are more limited, the adapted search string was: ("seaweed aquaculture"
                  OR "seaweed farming" OR "seaweed cultivation") AND ("Mekong Delta" OR Vietnam)
                  AND ("climate change" OR adaptation OR "ecosystem services" OR livelihood OR "value
                  chain" OR policy OR governance). Transferable international evidence was additionally
                  identified through thematic reference-list and citation searching without geographical
                  restrictions.
                    No lower publication-year restriction was imposed because earlier foundational stud-
                  ies and policy documents were retained when they remained relevant to the concep-
                  tual or regional analysis. The initial database searches were restricted to publications
Nguyen et al. Discover Environment   (2026) 4:530                                                                Page 4 of 20




                  in English and Vietnamese. Eligible sources included peer-reviewed research articles,
                  review articles, doctoral theses, and authoritative policy or technical reports. Publi-
                  cations were included when they provided substantive evidence on at least one of the
                  following topics: the current status of seaweed aquaculture; economic and livelihood
                  potential; ecosystem services and environmental effects; cultivation technologies; mar-
                  ket and value-chain development; climate adaptation; or institutional and policy con-
                  ditions. Duplicate records, publications unrelated to seaweed cultivation or sectoral
                  development, non-substantive web materials, and documents without sufficient infor-
                  mation for thematic analysis were excluded. Laboratory, biochemical, or taxonomic
                  studies were considered only when their findings were directly relevant to cultivation
                  suitability, value addition, product development, or sustainability assessment. Grey lit-
                  erature was not subjected to a formal risk-of-bias assessment tool; however, policy and
                  technical reports were appraised prior to inclusion based on source authority, method-
                  ological transparency, geographical relevance, and sufficient substantive detail for the-
                  matic synthesis.
                     The literature search identified 358 records, including 322 records from Scopus, Web
                  of Science, and Google Scholar and 36 additional records from institutional reposito-
                  ries, organizational websites, reference-list searching, and citation searching. Dupli-
                  cate records were identified using the duplicate-detection function in EndNote X8 and
                  subsequently verified manually by comparing titles, DOIs, author names, and publica-
                  tion years. After 79 duplicate records were removed, 279 records underwent title and
                  abstract screening. Of these, 160 records were excluded because they were clearly unre-
                  lated to the geographical or thematic scope of the review. The full texts of 119 potentially
                  relevant documents were subsequently assessed against the eligibility criteria. Sixty-two
                  documents were excluded because they were outside the geographical or thematic scope
                  (n = 25), had insufficient relevance to seaweed aquaculture development and sustain-
                  ability (n = 21), contained insufficient substantive information for thematic synthesis (n
                  = 10), or were unavailable in full text (n = 6). A final set of 57 sources was included in
                  the narrative synthesis. The review process is summarized in Fig. 1.
                     For each included source, information was extracted on geographical coverage, pub-
                  lication type, study or document characteristics, cultivation system or seaweed species
                  where applicable, principal findings, and relevance to the analytical framework. Evi-
                  dence was interpreted according to its methodological transparency, source authority,
                  geographical relevance, and consistency with the broader literature. During the thematic
                  synthesis, greater interpretive weight was given to peer-reviewed and Mekong Delta-
                  specific evidence, while Vietnam-level and international evidence was used cautiously
                  to address regional knowledge gaps. The evidence was synthesized thematically across
                  five areas: conceptual background; current development and potential; key challenges
                  and constraints; strategic solutions; and policy and research implications. Because the
                  included sources differed substantially in design, geographical setting, evidence type,
                  and reported outcomes, quantitative meta-analysis was not appropriate.
                     As a structured narrative review, this study may not have captured every relevant
                  publication, particularly locally published or non-indexed Vietnamese-language evi-
                  dence. The inclusion of Google Scholar and institutional grey literature improved
                  regional and policy coverage but introduced variation in source quality and reporting

                  3 Conceptual background: seaweed aquaculture and sustainability
                  3.1 Seaweed aquaculture as a nature‑based solution
                  Seaweed aquaculture is increasingly recognized as a nature-based solution that can
                  simultaneously address environmental degradation, climate change challenges, and live-
                  lihood sustainability [14]. In contrast to many conventional aquaculture systems that
                  depend on external feed and other resource-intensive inputs, seaweed cultivation is a
                  non-fed production system that requires little to no freshwater, fertilizer, or feed inputs,
                  making it comparatively low-impact and resource-efficient [20].
                     One of the most important ecological functions of seaweed is its capacity to
                  absorb carbon through photosynthesis and convert it into biomass, thereby contrib-
                  uting to climate change mitigation [15]. For quantitative context, Pessarrodona et al.
                  [15] used a more realistic carbon-fixation value of approximately 350 g C m⁻2 y⁻1
                  based on the mean global harvested yield. Seaweed cultivation has also been dis-
                  cussed as a potential pathway for enhancing blue carbon benefits in coastal ecosys-
                  tems; however, carbon fixation in cultivated biomass should not be equated directly
Nguyen et al. Discover Environment   (2026) 4:530                                                          Page 6 of 20




                  with long-term carbon sequestration, which remains highly context dependent [14,
                  21]. In addition, seaweed can remove excess nutrients, particularly nitrogen (N) and
                  phosphorus (P), from aquatic environments, thereby helping to mitigate eutrophica-
                  tion and improve water quality [22]. This function is especially relevant in coastal
                  areas affected by agricultural runoff and aquaculture effluents, where nutrient
                  enrichment has become a persistent environmental concern [23].
                    Seaweed farming may also contribute to coastal protection by attenuating wave
                  energy and supporting shoreline resilience [24, 25]. Model-based studies of sus-
                  pended kelp canopies have evaluated shallow-water depths of approximately 2–4 m,
                  with greater wave attenuation occurring in shallower water where the canopy occu-
                  pies a larger proportion of the water column. Effective configurations generally
                  involve dense longline canopies suspended near the surface, with longer and wider
                  blades providing greater interaction with wave-driven flow; however, the optimal
                  depth and canopy structure remain species- and site-specific [24, 25]. These ecosys-
                  tem-based functions are particularly important in low-lying and climate-vulnerable
                  coastal regions, where nature-based production systems can help strengthen both
                  ecological stability and adaptive capacity. In this sense, seaweed aquaculture should
                  not be understood merely as a source of biomass production, but as a multifunc-
                  tional coastal activity with broader environmental value.



                  3.2 Contribution to sustainable development
                  Beyond its ecological functions, seaweed aquaculture also contributes to the broader
                  goals of sustainable development across economic, social, and environmental
                  dimensions [14, 18]. Economically, seaweed farming offers opportunities for income
                  diversification, particularly for small-scale coastal households that are increasingly
                  exposed to climate-related disruptions affecting conventional agriculture and aqua-
                  culture [16]. Its relatively low capital requirements and short production cycles may
                  make it a more accessible livelihood option than many other forms of aquaculture
                  [16]. In addition, rising demand for seaweed-based products, including food ingredi-
                  ents, pharmaceuticals, and bio-based materials, creates scope for value addition and
                  market expansion [26].
                    From a social perspective, seaweed aquaculture may strengthen livelihood resil-
                  ience by reducing dependence on a narrow set of climate-sensitive activities [27]. It
                  can also support gender inclusion, as women often participate actively in cultivation,
                  post-harvest handling, processing, and trading across seaweed value chains [28].
                  Such participation may generate wider community benefits and contribute to local
                  adaptive capacity, especially in vulnerable coastal settings [14].
                    Environmentally, seaweed farming provides multiple ecosystem services, includ-
                  ing water purification, habitat support for marine organisms, and carbon regulation
                  [18]. These functions can improve ecological health while also supporting the sus-
                  tainability of adjacent aquaculture and fisheries systems. Taken together, these con-
                  tributions suggest that seaweed aquaculture has the potential to function not only
                  as an economic activity, but also as a strategic pathway toward more resilient and
                  sustainable coastal development.

                  3.3 Integrated analytical framework
                  To examine the role of seaweed aquaculture in sustainable development more system-
                  atically, an integrated analytical framework is needed to connect climate pressures,
                  production responses, and sustainability outcomes [14, 29]. In this review, seaweed
                  aquaculture is conceptualized as a pathway through which climate change pressures
                  shape sector development and, in turn, influence economic, social, and environmen-
                  tal sustainability outcomes (Fig. 2).
                    Within this framework, climate change is understood not only as a source of vul-
                  nerability, but also as a driver of adaptation and innovation in coastal production sys-
                  tems [30]. Seaweed aquaculture is therefore framed as a potential adaptive response
                  through which environmental constraints may be transformed into opportunities
                  for more sustainable forms of production and livelihood development. Its outcomes
                  may be reflected in improved income diversification, stronger social resilience, and
                  enhanced environmental performance [14].
                    At the same time, the development of seaweed aquaculture does not depend on
                  climate and ecological conditions alone. Its expansion is also shaped by technologi-
                  cal capacity, market opportunities, and institutional support. Institutional support
                  can moderate these sustainability outcomes through different pathways. Economi-
                  cally, clear regulations, extension services, market coordination, and access to finance
                  can reduce investment uncertainty and improve producers’ ability to capture value,
                  whereas environmentally, effective zoning, monitoring, and production standards can
                  help control ecological risks and strengthen the delivery of ecosystem services [29].
                  For this reason, the framework adopted in this study provides a foundation for ana-
                  lysing seaweed aquaculture not simply as a biological or technical activity, but as a
                  socio-ecological development pathway. The analytical relationships discussed above
                  are summarized in Fig. 2, which illustrates how climate change pressures shape sea-
                  weed aquaculture development and how sustainability outcomes are mediated by
                  technological, market, and institutional conditions. Building on this analytical frame-
                  work, the following section examines the current development status and the eco-
                  nomic and environmental potential of seaweed aquaculture in the Mekong Delta.

                  4 Current development and potential in the Mekong Delta
                  4.1 Current status
                  Compared with dominant aquaculture activities such as shrimp and fish farming, sea-
                  weed aquaculture in the Mekong Delta remains at an early stage of development.
                  Although Vietnam cultivates several commercially important seaweed species, includ-
                  ing Kappaphycus alvarezii, Kappaphycus striatus, Eucheuma denticulatum, and several
                  Gracilaria taxa, large-scale production is still concentrated mainly outside the Delta [19,
                  31]. Within the Mekong Delta, current evidence most clearly documents the cultivation
                  and management of the red seaweed Gracilaria tenuistipitata, particularly in improved-
                  extensive shrimp and shrimp–crab pond systems [32, 33]. Other locally occurring mac-
                  roalgae, including Cladophoraceae and Enteromorpha spp., are also found in shrimp
                  ponds but are generally naturally occurring rather than established commercial crops
                  [33]. Accordingly, Kappaphycus and Eucheuma species are better regarded as candidate
                  taxa for future regional expansion than as major cultivated crops currently established in
                  the Delta.
                    Where seaweed cultivation has been adopted, it is commonly integrated into shrimp-
                  based farming systems, where it may improve resource efficiency and provide additional
                  ecological functions [32]. However, these practices remain limited in scale and are not
                  yet supported by sufficiently strong technical standardization, extension services, or
                  development programs [19, 33]. Available evidence from the Mekong Delta primarily
                  documents naturally occurring Gracilaria tenuistipitata biomass in improved-extensive
                  shrimp ponds [33]. Vegetative propagation through retained thallus material is techni-
                  cally feasible for this species [34], but the reviewed literature did not identify dedicated
                  commercial seaweed nurseries or hatcheries operating at regional scale in the Delta. As
                  a result, seaweed aquaculture has so far made only a modest contribution to the wider
                  aquaculture economy of the Mekong Delta, despite its potentially strategic relevance in
                  the context of climate change and coastal livelihood transition.



                  4.2 Economic potential
                  Despite its currently limited scale, seaweed aquaculture has considerable economic
                  potential in the Mekong Delta, particularly through value addition and closer integra-
                  tion with expanding global seaweed markets [26]. Seaweed biomass can be processed
                  into high-value hydrocolloids such as agar and carrageenan, which are widely used in
                  food, pharmaceutical, and industrial applications [35]. Seaweeds are also increasingly
                  recognized as sources of bioactive compounds for functional foods, nutraceuticals, and
                  pharmaceutical products [36]. In Vietnam, seaweed products currently serve domestic
                  markets for human food and animal feed and are also used as raw materials for cosmet-
                  ics and traditional medicine, while a proportion of production is directed toward inter-
                  national markets [19]. At the same time, growing demand for sustainable and bio-based
                  materials is creating new market opportunities for seaweed-producing regions [26].
                    In the Mekong Delta, these trends suggest that the economic value of seaweed
                  aquaculture lies not only in primary production, but also in its downstream process-
                  ing and market diversification potential. Stronger investment in processing technolo-
                  gies, quality assurance systems, and certification could improve competitiveness and
Nguyen et al. Discover Environment   (2026) 4:530                                                              Page 9 of 20




                  facilitate entry into higher-value market segments [26]. At the local level, seaweed
                  farming may also contribute to employment and income generation. Small-scale
                  nearshore or pond-based cultivation may have relatively modest entry costs com-
                  pared with many fed aquaculture systems [16]. However, transition toward com-
                  mercially scaled production requires substantial investment in farm infrastructure,
                  quality seedlings, post-harvest handling, processing facilities, certification, and mar-
                  ket development [26]. In this regard, seaweed aquaculture may serve both as a live-
                  lihood diversification strategy and as an emerging component of a broader coastal
                  bioeconomy.



                  4.3 Environmental potential
                  In addition to its economic prospects, seaweed aquaculture offers significant environ-
                  mental potential that is highly relevant to the ecological challenges of the Mekong Delta
                  [18]. One of its most important functions is the removal of excess nutrients, particularly
                  N and P, from aquaculture effluents and agricultural runoff, thereby improving water
                  quality and supporting the ecological sustainability of surrounding aquatic systems [22].
                  In laboratory experiments, nutrient uptake rates reported for Gracilaria tenuistipi-
                  tata var. liui reached approximately 0.32–0.36 μmol N g⁻1 h⁻1 and 0.017–0.018 μmol P
                  g⁻1 h⁻1 under favorable light conditions. Nutrient uptake varied with environmental fac-
                  tors including light intensity, pH, temperature, and salinity, with higher N and P uptake
                  observed at temperatures of 21–26 °C [37]. By assimilating dissolved nutrients, seaweed
                  cultivation may help reduce eutrophication in intensive farming areas and lower the risk
                  of harmful algal blooms and related ecosystem degradation [23].
                    Seaweed aquaculture may also contribute to both climate change mitigation and
                  adaptation through carbon uptake and climate-regulating functions [15]. In addi-
                  tion, its physical structure can attenuate wave energy and enhance coastal resilience,
                  which is particularly valuable in low-lying coastal areas exposed to sea-level rise, ero-
                  sion, and extreme weather events [24]. For the Mekong Delta, where environmental
                  degradation and climate vulnerability are increasingly intertwined, these ecosystem-
                  based functions strengthen the case for considering seaweed aquaculture not only
                  as a productive activity, but also as part of a broader strategy for sustainable coastal
                  management. Overall, the region possesses substantial yet underutilized potential for
                  seaweed aquaculture, and realizing this potential will depend on the effective align-
                  ment of technological innovation, market development, and policy support.



                  5 Key challenges and constraints
                  Despite its considerable promise, the development of seaweed aquaculture in the
                  Mekong Delta is constrained by a set of interrelated environmental, economic, insti-
                  tutional, and technological barriers. These constraints not only limit the scalability of
                  the sector, but also undermine its long-term sustainability, productivity, and competi-
                  tiveness. Understanding these challenges is therefore essential for assessing whether
                  seaweed aquaculture can realistically evolve into a viable climate-resilient develop-
                  ment pathway in the region.

                  5.1 Environmental constraints
                  Environmental pressures represent one of the most significant constraints on the expan-
                  sion of seaweed aquaculture in the Mekong Delta. A major concern is the degradation of
                  mangrove ecosystems associated with aquaculture expansion, particularly shrimp farm-
                  ing [13]. The conversion of mangrove forests into aquaculture ponds has contributed to
                  biodiversity loss, weakened coastal protection, and increased greenhouse gas emissions,
                  highlighting the trade-off between short-term economic gains and long-term ecologi-
                  cal sustainability [38]. In addition, water pollution and chemical runoff from intensive
                  agriculture and aquaculture pose serious risks to seaweed cultivation [39]. The discharge
                  of nutrients, antibiotics, and pesticides into coastal waters can degrade water quality
                  and disrupt surrounding ecosystems, especially in densely farmed areas where water
                  exchange is limited and environmental monitoring remains weak [39]. These conditions
                  may directly affect seaweed growth and overall farm performance. Moreover, because
                  environmental contaminants, including pesticides and pharmaceuticals, can attach to or
                  be taken up by seaweeds, agrochemical and antibiotic residues in exchanged pond water
                  could compromise tissue quality and food safety [40].
                     The Mekong Delta is also highly exposed to climate-related stressors, including salin-
                  ity fluctuations, temperature variability, and extreme weather events [41]. For Gracilaria
                  tenuistipitata, the best-documented seaweed in local shrimp-based systems [32, 33],
                  however, a locally validated upper thermal tolerance threshold has not yet been estab-
                  lished. Consequently, the available regional evidence is insufficient to determine whether
                  seasonal temperature peaks consistently exceed its physiological tolerance, although epi-
                  sodic heat stress may still reduce growth and cultivation stability. Such factors can alter
                  seaweed physiology, reduce biomass yield, affect biochemical composition, and increase
                  susceptibility to disease. As a result, environmental instability introduces significant
                  uncertainty into cultivation systems and weakens production reliability, particularly for
                  small-scale producers with limited adaptive capacity.

                  5.2 Economic and financial barriers
                  From an economic perspective, the development of seaweed aquaculture in the Mekong
                  Delta is constrained by several structural barriers. One of the most important is limited
                  access to capital and credit among small-scale producers, which restricts investment in
                  cultivation infrastructure, quality seedlings, and post-harvest processing [16]. Formal
                  collateral requirements can further restrict smallholders’ access to credit, thereby lim-
                  iting their capacity to invest in separate inlet and outlet canals, pumps, reservoirs, and
                  filtration structures needed to improve pond-level water exchange and biosecurity [42].
                  High initial costs for farm establishment, equipment, and processing facilities may fur-
                  ther discourage new entrants and limit the expansion of existing operations [26].
                     These financial constraints are compounded by market uncertainty, including price
                  fluctuations, unstable demand in some commodity segments, and strong competi-
                  tion from established seaweed-producing countries [26]. Another major challenge is
                  the weakness and fragmentation of seaweed value chains, as poor coordination among
                  producers, processors, traders, and buyers reduces value addition and weakens farm-
                  ers’ bargaining power [26]. In many cases, producers remain dependent on interme-
                  diaries, which can compress profit margins and make income streams less stable and
Nguyen et al. Discover Environment   (2026) 4:530                                                               Page 11 of 20




                  predictable. Taken together, these barriers limit the economic attractiveness of seaweed
                  aquaculture, especially in the absence of stronger downstream market support.

                  5.3 Institutional and policy gaps
                  Institutional and policy-related barriers also significantly constrain the development of
                  the seaweed sector in the Mekong Delta [19]. One key issue is the lack of clear spatial
                  planning and coherent governance arrangements for coastal aquaculture activities. At
                  the local level, responsibilities for coastal aquaculture zoning and sea-area management
                  are shared among provincial People’s Committees, provincial Departments of Agricul-
                  ture and Environment through their fisheries and marine–environmental management
                  functions, and commune-level People’s Committees for certain sea-area allocation and
                  management responsibilities. This distribution of responsibilities can create coordina-
                  tion gaps in spatial planning, licensing, environmental management, and resource allo-
                  cation, thereby discouraging investment and hindering long-term sector development
                  [43]. In addition, technical standardization and operational guidance for safe produc-
                  tion, quality assurance, and risk management remain insufficient in emerging seaweed
                  value chains [43]. This limits producers’ ability to meet market requirements, maintain
                  consistent quality, and secure stable off-take agreements. Policy support for seaweed
                  aquaculture also remains relatively limited compared with more established aquacul-
                  ture sectors, reducing its attractiveness to both smallholders and private investors [19].
                  More broadly, seaweed aquaculture is still only partially integrated into marine spatial
                  planning, climate adaptation policy, and coastal management strategies. This weak pol-
                  icy integration reduces the sector’s visibility in public planning and limits access to the
                  institutional support needed for sustainable expansion. For the Mekong Delta, where
                  climate adaptation and coastal governance are increasingly interconnected, such policy
                  gaps represent a major constraint on scaling seaweed aquaculture in a coordinated and
                  strategic manner.

                  5.4 Knowledge and technology gaps
                  A further barrier to the sustainable expansion of seaweed aquaculture lies in the lim-
                  ited availability of scientific knowledge and technological innovation tailored to local
                  conditions [19]. Existing research on seaweed cultivation in the Mekong Delta remains
                  limited, particularly with regard to optimal farming techniques, species selection, and
                  carrying-capacity assessment [31, 43]. This restricts the evidence base needed to support
                  appropriate farm design and site-specific management decisions.
                    There is also still insufficient understanding of the ecological implications and long-
                  term sustainability of large-scale seaweed farming, especially in relation to surround-
                  ing ecosystems and local environmental performance [44]. In addition, the potential of
                  integrated systems such as integrated multi-trophic aquaculture (IMTA) remains under-
                  explored in the regional context, despite their documented benefits in other settings
                  [31, 45]. Equally important is the limited transfer of technology and practical knowl-
                  edge to producers. Weak extension systems and insufficient training support can slow
                  the adoption of improved farming practices, reduce productivity gains, and widen the
                  gap between research and practice [46]. As a result, technological constraints in the
                  Mekong Delta are not simply a matter of innovation availability, but also of adaptation,
Nguyen et al. Discover Environment   (2026) 4:530                                                              Page 12 of 20




                  dissemination, and local implementation capacity. Overall, addressing these knowledge
                  and technology gaps will require stronger connections among research institutions,
                  extension services, producers, and policymakers.


                  6 Strategic solutions for sustainable development
                  Unlocking the full potential of seaweed aquaculture in the Mekong Delta will require a
                  coordinated and multidimensional strategy. Because the barriers facing the sector are
                  environmental, economic, institutional, and technological in nature, the correspond-
                  ing solutions must also be integrated across these domains. Sustainable development
                  of the sector therefore depends not on a single intervention, but on the alignment of
                  innovation, market development, governance support, capacity building, and climate
                  adaptation.


                  6.1 Technological innovations
                  Technological innovation is a key prerequisite for improving productivity, resilience, and
                  environmental performance in seaweed aquaculture [47]. One important priority is the
                  development of disease- and climate-tolerant strains that can maintain yield stability
                  under salinity fluctuations, temperature stress, and pathogen pressure [41]. Advances in
                  selection, priming, and breeding technologies offer significant potential in this regard
                  [41]. At the farm level, the adoption of improved cultivation practices is equally impor-
                  tant. Optimized seeding methods, site-specific farm design, and better harvest timing
                  can increase production efficiency while reducing environmental risks [47]. A particu-
                  larly promising pathway is the expansion of IMTA, in which seaweed is co-cultivated
                  with species such as shrimp or fish [17]. In such systems, seaweed functions as a natural
                  biofilter by absorbing excess nutrients and improving water quality, thereby enhancing
                  both production efficiency and ecological sustainability [17]. When IMTA is imple-
                  mented with shrimp, biosecurity should include the use of health-screened stocking
                  material, quarantine of new stocks where appropriate, filtration or screening of incom-
                  ing water, routine health monitoring, and cleaning and disinfection of shared equipment
                  to minimize pathogen introduction and cross-transmission among cultured components
                  [45]. For the Mekong Delta, where shrimp-based systems are already widespread, the
                  promotion of IMTA may provide a practical route toward more circular and resource-
                  efficient aquaculture models.


                  6.2 Value chain development
                  Strengthening the seaweed value chain is essential for improving economic returns and
                  market competitiveness [26]. A central priority is to improve linkages among farmers,
                  processors, traders, and exporters, as better coordination can reduce inefficiencies,
                  improve market transparency, and increase the bargaining power of primary producers
                  [26]. Without stronger vertical and horizontal connections across the value chain, pro-
                  ducers are likely to remain locked into low-value and unstable market positions.
                    Promoting value-added products is another critical strategy. Rather than relying
                  primarily on raw or minimally processed seaweed, investment should support pro-
                  cessing technologies that enable higher-value outputs such as hydrocolloids, func-
                  tional foods, pharmaceuticals, and bio-based materials [26, 35]. This shift could
Nguyen et al. Discover Environment   (2026) 4:530                                                                Page 13 of 20




                  improve profit margins, diversify market outlets, and reduce dependence on lower-
                  value commodity segments. In addition, branding and certification systems, including
                  eco-labels and sustainability standards, can strengthen product credibility, facilitate
                  market access, and improve compliance with quality and traceability requirements
                  [48]. In the Mekong Delta, such measures would be particularly important for mov-
                  ing the sector from small-scale supplementary production toward a more competitive
                  and commercially viable development model.



                  6.3 Policy and governance improvements
                  Effective policy and governance frameworks are essential for creating an enabling
                  environment for sustainable seaweed development [49]. One of the most immediate
                  priorities is the establishment of clear spatial planning and aquaculture zoning, which
                  can reduce resource-use conflicts, improve spatial efficiency, and encourage long-
                  term investment [43]. For a coastal region such as the Mekong Delta, where multiple
                  production systems and environmental pressures intersect, spatial clarity is especially
                  important.
                    Governments should also introduce targeted financial incentives and support
                  mechanisms, including concessional credit, grants for technology adoption, and tai-
                  lored assistance for smallholder producers, in order to lower entry barriers and stim-
                  ulate investment across the sector [50]. At the same time, seaweed aquaculture should
                  be more explicitly integrated into national and regional climate adaptation strategies,
                  given its potential role as a nature-based solution within coastal development policy
                  [50]. Better integration with broader marine spatial planning and coastal management
                  frameworks would improve policy coherence, expand access to climate and develop-
                  ment finance, and support more strategic sectoral scaling. In this sense, governance
                  reform is not simply a matter of regulation, but of creating an institutional environ-
                  ment in which seaweed aquaculture can develop with clearer direction and stronger
                  public support.



                  6.4 Capacity building and knowledge transfer
                  Human capacity development is a critical component of sustainable sector growth [14].
                  Training programs for farmers can strengthen technical skills in cultivation, harvesting,
                  post-harvest handling, and farm management, thereby supporting higher productivity
                  and more consistent product quality [50]. In emerging sectors such as seaweed aquacul-
                  ture in the Mekong Delta, such capacity building is particularly important because many
                  producers may have limited prior experience with specialized farming and processing
                  techniques. Participatory research approaches that involve farmers, researchers, and
                  local stakeholders can also facilitate the co-creation of knowledge and help adapt tech-
                  nological innovations to local conditions [51]. In parallel, stronger extension systems
                  are needed to bridge the gap between research and practice. Effective extension can
                  disseminate best practices, provide technical guidance, and encourage the adoption of
                  improved technologies at scale [52]. Taken together, these measures can help ensure that
                  innovation is not only developed, but also effectively translated into field-level practice.

                  6.5 Climate adaptation strategies
                  Given the high climate vulnerability of the Mekong Delta, adaptive strategies are essen-
                  tial to the long-term sustainability of seaweed aquaculture [30]. One key measure is the
                  careful selection of cultivation sites based on environmental factors such as salinity, tem-
                  perature, water depth, and nutrient availability, as site suitability strongly influences both
                  productivity and risk exposure [53]. In this respect, site-selection planning should be
                  treated as a foundational step rather than a purely technical afterthought.
                    The adoption of seasonal and adaptive farming practices is also important. Adjust-
                  ments in cultivation timing, species choice, and farm management can improve resil-
                  ience to climate variability and reduce production losses under changing environmental
                  conditions. In addition, ecosystem-based management approaches can help integrate
                  seaweed aquaculture into broader coastal systems by linking cultivation with mangrove
                  conservation, water-quality protection, and the sustainable use of marine resources [54].
                  Such approaches are particularly relevant in the Mekong Delta, where the long-term
                  viability of aquaculture depends on the health and resilience of interconnected coastal
                  ecosystems. Overall, the sustainable development of seaweed aquaculture in the Mekong
                  Delta requires a holistic strategy that combines technological innovation, value chain
                  development, policy reform, capacity building, and climate adaptation. Only through
                  the integration of these elements can seaweed farming evolve into a resilient, competi-

                  7 Policy implications and research outlook
                  7.1 Policy implications
                  The findings of this review suggest that seaweed aquaculture should be recognized as a
                  strategic sector within the green and blue economy, particularly in climate-vulnerable
                  coastal regions such as the Mekong Delta [14, 55]. Given its multifunctional capacity to
                  generate economic benefits, deliver ecosystem services, and support climate adaptation,
                  seaweed farming can contribute meaningfully to more sustainable coastal development
                  pathways [14]. To realize this potential, seaweed aquaculture should be more system-
                  atically integrated into coastal management frameworks, including aquaculture zoning,
                  marine resource governance, and coordination with mangrove conservation and resto-
                  ration efforts [43]. Such integration would help reduce resource-use conflicts, improve
                  environmental outcomes, and support longer-term sector development. In addition,
                  seaweed aquaculture should be incorporated more explicitly into national and regional
                  climate adaptation strategies, particularly in recognition of its role as a nature-based
                  solution [50].
                    Policy frameworks should therefore move beyond general support and instead provide
                  targeted instruments for sector development. These may include financial incentives,
                  technical assistance, and institutional mechanisms that facilitate public–private partner-
                  ships, producer participation, and community-based implementation [50]. Within the
                  Mekong Delta, a coherent and forward-looking policy framework is especially impor-
                  tant, as the long-term viability of seaweed aquaculture depends not only on biological
                  feasibility, but also on the alignment of governance, investment, and coastal adaptation
                  planning. In this sense, seaweed aquaculture should be viewed not as a marginal sup-
                  plementary activity, but as a potentially strategic component of climate-resilient coastal
                  transformation.

                  7.2 Limitations of the current evidence base and future research outlook
                  The current evidence base on seaweed aquaculture in the Mekong Delta remains lim-
                  ited, fragmented, and uneven across environmental, economic, social, and institutional
                  dimensions. Much of the available knowledge on ecosystem services, market develop-
                  ment, and cultivation technologies has been derived from studies conducted in other
                  coastal regions. Although these findings provide useful comparative insights, their direct
                  applicability to the ecological conditions, production systems, market structures, and
                  governance context of the Mekong Delta has not yet been sufficiently validated. In addi-
                  tion, the lack of long-term field studies, standardized assessment methods, and locally
                  grounded economic data limits the extent to which the productivity, environmental
                  performance, and scalability of seaweed aquaculture can currently be assessed with
                  confidence.
                    Against this background, several research priorities should be addressed to support
                  evidence-based policymaking and sustainable sector development. First, more robust
                  quantification of the ecosystem services provided by seaweed farming is needed, par-
                  ticularly with regard to carbon-related climate benefits, nutrient removal, and coastal
Nguyen et al. Discover Environment   (2026) 4:530                                                                Page 17 of 20




                  protection [56]. Such evidence is essential for determining whether, and under what
                  conditions, these benefits can be incorporated into environmental accounting, policy
                  evaluation, climate-finance mechanisms, and investment decisions. Further research
                  should also distinguish short-term carbon uptake by cultivated biomass from long-term
                  carbon sequestration to avoid overstating the potential climate-mitigation contribution
                  of seaweed farming.
                    Second, improved economic valuation approaches are required to capture both the
                  market and non-market benefits of seaweed aquaculture. A more comprehensive valu-
                  ation framework could clarify the sector’s broader contribution to sustainable develop-
                  ment while providing a stronger basis for public investment, financial incentives, and
                  policy integration [57]. Future assessments should consider not only production reve-
                  nues, but also establishment and operating costs, market volatility, processing require-
                  ments, ecosystem-service benefits, livelihood effects, and the distribution of costs and
                  benefits among farmers, processors, communities, and other stakeholders.
                    Third, more research is needed on the long-term environmental effects of seaweed cul-
                  tivation. Although seaweed farming may provide nutrient-removal and habitat-related
                  benefits, large-scale expansion could also generate unintended ecological consequences,
                  including changes in nutrient cycling, light availability, hydrodynamic conditions, habi-
                  tat structure, and interactions with surrounding species. These potential effects should
                  be examined through longitudinal, site-specific, and system-based assessments before
                  extensive cultivation is promoted [44]. Particular attention should also be given to carry-
                  ing capacity, site suitability, species selection, disease risks, and the cumulative impacts
                  of seaweed farms when combined with other coastal production activities.
                    Finally, the conditions under which IMTA systems can be adapted and scaled up in
                  the Mekong Delta require closer examination. In these systems, seaweed is co-cultivated
                  with species such as shrimp or fish to recycle dissolved nutrients and improve resource-
                  use efficiency. However, their wider adoption depends on locally demonstrated techni-
                  cal feasibility, economic viability, environmental performance, farmer acceptance, and
                  institutional support [17]. Future studies should therefore evaluate appropriate species
                  combinations, farm configurations, stocking and harvesting practices, biosecurity meas-
                  ures, and governance arrangements under the salinity, temperature, hydrological, and
                  production conditions of the Mekong Delta.
                    The future development of seaweed aquaculture in the region will consequently
                  depend on the generation of a stronger, locally validated, and interdisciplinary evidence
                  base. Priority should be given to long-term field experiments, pilot-scale production
                  models, comparative economic assessments, carrying-capacity evaluations, and partici-
                  patory studies involving farmers, researchers, industry actors, and policymakers. Such
                  research would help determine where seaweed aquaculture is environmentally appro-
                  priate, economically viable, and socially acceptable. In parallel, technological innovation
                  should be aligned with market development and institutional support so that promis-
                  ing cultivation models can progress beyond isolated or small-scale initiatives. Rather
                  than assuming that seaweed aquaculture is universally suitable across the Mekong Delta,
                  future research and policy should identify the specific locations, production systems,
                  value chains, and governance conditions under which it can contribute effectively to cli-
                  mate-resilient and inclusive coastal development.
