Abstract
Since the late 1980s to 2025, at least 16 disease entities have been reported to affect com-
mercially cultivated seaweeds, occurring in both open-water farming systems and nursery
facilities. The expansion and intensification of seaweed production have increased the fre-
quency of host–pathogen interactions, a trend further exacerbated by environmental stress-
ors associated with climate change, including ocean warming, salinity fluctuations, and
acidification. These conditions have contributed to rising disease incidence, with conse-
quences such as reduced yields and product quality, greater economic losses, and a poten-
tial weakening of the overall resilience of the seaweed industry. This review was conducted
using a systematic literature review approach by compiling and screening scientific publi-
cations from Scopus, Web of Science, SpringerLink, and two additional relevant sources to
provide a comprehensive synthesis. Based on the evidence assembled, this review aims to
(i) map the relationships between biotic and abiotic factors and the emergence and spatial
distribution of diseases across seaweed species and cultivation regions, (ii) examine the
economic consequences of disease along the seaweed value chain, and (iii) evaluate key
aspects of disease management in seaweed aquaculture systems. Overall, this review pro-
vides a scientific basis for shaping research and policy agendas that prioritise risk-based
biosecurity, the development of tolerant varieties, early-warning systems and rapid diag-
nostics, high-resolution environmental monitoring, and strengthened collaborative govern-
ance through public–private partnerships to support sustainable and disease-resilient sea-
weed aquaculture.

Introduction

The global seaweed cultivation sector has expanded in response to growing demand
from various industries, such as food, cosmetics, pharmaceuticals, biomedicine, derma-
tology, agriculture, and fisheries (Polat et al. 2023; Debbarma et al. 2024). Currently,
the largest reference database for seaweed taxonomy (www.​algae​base.​org) recognises
over 12 thousand seaweed species (Vigil et al. 2024), with more than 291 species being
commercially cultivated worldwide (Iqbal 2022). Among these, brown seaweeds (Lami-
naria/Saccharina, Undaria, Sargassum), red seaweeds (Kappaphycus sp., Eucheuma
sp., Gracilaria, Pyropia/Porphyra), and green seaweeds (Ulva and Caulerpa) are the
most commonly farmed species (Iqbal 2022). In 2021, global seaweed production
amounted to 35.1 million tonnes in live weight, with 94% of the total yield concentrated
in four primary countries: China contributed 60%, Indonesia 25%, South Korea 5%,
and the Philippines 4% (UNCTAD 2024). Embracing this development, the global sea-
weed market is projected to reach USD 17.8 billion by 2032 (Spagnuolo and Genovese
2024). Furthermore, seaweed is projected to play a crucial role in meeting the needs of
a global population expected to exceed 9 billion by 2050 (Duarte et al. 2022). However,
based on the systematic literature review method we applied, it is evident that numerous
reports of disease incidents have been published from the late 1980s to 2025 (Table 1),
reflecting a wide range of reported symptoms (Table 2). Diseases caused by pathogens
such as bacteria, parasites, and viruses are becoming more prevalent, prompted by envi-
ronmental stressors like fluctuations in salinity and rising seawater temperatures (Kim
et al. 2023; Dekker et al. 2024; Zhuang et al. 2024). These stressors compromise the
seaweed’s capacity to produce antimicrobial agents, leaving it more susceptible to colo-
nisation and infection by opportunistic pathogens (Largo et al. 2024). Furthermore, as
cultivation intensifies and expands, the incidence and diversity of diseases in farmed
seaweed species have increased and are widely documented in various regions (Fig. 1).
    Despite growing concerns over disease outbreaks, several countries continue to set
ambitious targets to become global leaders in seaweed production, recognising its poten-
tial as a key strategy for sustaining the sector and contributing to global food security over
the next 30 years (Bhuyan 2023). Culinary demand spans taxa across climates, includ-
ing P. palmata (traditionally eaten as dulse in the UK), S. japonica (haidai in Chinese
or kombu in Japanese), U. pinnatifida (wakame), Alaria esculenta (alaria), Pyropia spp.
(nori), Sargassum fusiforme (hijiki), and C. lentillifera (umibudō) (Mukhia et al. 2024).
In tropical waters, beyond C. lentillifera, one of the most commercially prominent com-
modities is Kappaphycus spp., a carrageenophyte eucheumatoid, is widely employed as
a thickener and gelling agent in jelly confectionery, candied products, crackers, jams, and
seaweed meatball-like product (Poeloengasih et al. 2014). In line with these opportuni-
ties, Indonesia aims to dominate the global edible seaweed market through the roadmap
set by Presidential Decree No. 33/2019, with eucheumatoids as the priority (Jompa et al.
2024). Concurrently, Malaysia revitalises aquaculture by promoting seaweeds as a primary
future source of food and alternative protein (Ismail et al. 2024). This potential is further
validated by evidence highlighting the high essential amino acid content in red seaweed
(Gracilaria), the high polyunsaturated fatty acid levels and low saturated fatty acid content
in brown seaweed (Sargassum) (Chin et al. 2023), and the significant protein levels in red
seaweed (Pyropia), a widely cultivated species in temperate zones, which are comparable
to the standards set by the World Health Organization (WHO) and the Food and Agricul-
ture Organization (FAO) (Machado et al. 2020).

sources from Google Scholar and FAO, published up to July 2025. This review aims to
deliver robust scientific insights into disease threats in seaweed aquaculture and to pro-

Publication screening

This systematic review was reported in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines as artic-
ulated by Charlton et al. (2016) for reporting systematic literature reviews. Literature
searches were conducted in three core databases: Scopus, Web of Science, and Springer-
Link, and supplemented by searches via Google Scholar and online resources including
the website of the FAO using the same keyword strategy. The search strategy employed
Boolean operators, and search settings are detailed in Table 3. The temporal scope was lim-
ited to peer-reviewed articles published in English up to July 2025.
    Inclusion criteria required publications to be reviews, reports, or research articles that
documented disease occurrence in seaweed populations at nursery units or during active
farming. A case was deemed eligible when (i) diseased seaweeds originated from nursery
or farming settings, (ii) the disease name was stated, (iii) the location of occurrence was
clearly reported, (iv) symptoms were recorded during the nursery or farming phase, and (v)
at least one form of verification was provided through macroscopic, microscopic, or molec-
ular assessment supported by photographic documentation and/or symptom descriptions.
Laboratory follow-up studies were retained when plant material originated from nurseries
or farms currently or previously affected by disease rather than from experimental induc-
tion. Publications were excluded if they were reviews lacking specification of the disease
name, the infected seaweed species, the identification method, and a clearly defined loca-
tion, or if diseased material derived from controlled experiments with induced disease that
did not reflect field conditions.
    Given the breadth of search terms required to identify articles on disease occurrence,
the initial database queries yielded a large number of records. Irrelevant subject areas were
excluded using database filtering functions, as summarised in Table 4.
    All search records were exported to EndNote X7 for systematic deduplication. Study
selection proceeded with title and abstract screening to apply the inclusion–exclusion crite-
ria consistently, followed by full-text eligibility assessment. Two reviewers (A.W.F.A. and
K.K.) conducted these steps independently to reduce selection bias, with disagreements
resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. In total, 75 articles met the crite-
ria and were included in the final synthesis, as summarised in Fig. 2. Data extraction was
performed in a structured manner for each article, capturing the cultivated seaweed species,
farming location, reported causal factors (abiotic and biotic), approaches to disease iden-
tification, and farming methods. The extracted data are presented in Tables 1 and 2, and
a spatial map of disease distribution across farming sites is shown in Fig. 1. These proce-
dures ensure that the search, selection, and synthesis align with best practice in systematic
reviews and provide a robust foundation for subsequent thematic analyses.

Biotic and abiotic drivers of disease in cultivated seaweeds

Numerous reports of diseases in cultivated seaweed have occurred across multiple regions
(see Table 1), in parallel with the rapid expansion of aquaculture and with variability in
the marine environment, including fluctuations in temperature, salinity, acidification, nutri-
ent availability, light intensity, and current dynamics, which together have amplified the
frequency and spatial extent of pathogen outbreaks (Ren et al. 2022; Li et al. 2023; Zhang
et al. 2024b). From a biotic perspective, numerous studies have documented the involve-
ment of bacteria, parasites, and viruses across commercial seaweed species (Friedlander
and Critchley 2024), whereas abiotic factors modulate host susceptibility through physio-
logical stress and shifts in the microbiome on the surface as well as within tissues (Ghade-
riardakani et al. 2020; Martínez et al. 2024). Within this framework, a microbial invasion is
considered pathogenic when effective contact with the host triggers abnormal physiologi-
cal processes that disrupt morphology, growth, performance, and reproductive success of
seaweeds, either through direct interactions or tissue penetration, accompanied by variable
host defence responses (Hurtado et al. 2019; Lang et al. 2024).




Biotic drivers

Bacteria

Associations between bacteria and seaweeds are ubiquitous: bacteria occur as epibionts
on the thallus or as endobionts within tissues, often assembling into surface biofilms, and
together with the host they constitute a holobiont characterised by chemical signalling and
nutrient exchange (Lu et al. 2023; Saha et al. 2024; Umashree et al. 2024). Bacterial pres-
ence on seaweeds is highly diverse and is typically shaped by host species and environmen-
tal conditions (Kaur et al. 2023). Nevertheless, numerous studies show that Proteobacteria
and Bacteroidetes dominate the microbiomes of red seaweeds (Xie et al. 2024), brown sea-
weeds (Ihua et al. 2020), and green seaweeds (Califano et al. 2020). Community composi-
tion is dynamic: environmental drivers such as temperature, nutrients, light, and season
modulate the relative abundance of bacterial groups (Lu et al. 2023). In addition, the diver-
sity of bacterial communities is influenced by seaweed exudates (for example monosac-
charides, amino acids, and polysaccharides), which play a key role in structuring commu-
nities and shaping gene-expression patterns. Epiphytic bacteria are enriched for genes that
degrade algal exudates and host cell-wall polysaccharides, including algL (alginate lyase)
and agarase (Pfister et al. 2025). Chemical interactions between seaweed exudates and bac-
teria induce the expression of substrate-specific transport and metabolic genes in bacteria
(Ahmed et al. 2021). In summary, bacteria exploit seaweed-derived nutrients as sources
of carbon and energy, activating genetic pathways for the uptake and degradation of host-
secreted organic compounds (Pfister et al. 2025).
    These bacteria can contribute to the seaweed life cycle by producing regulatory com-
pounds and secondary metabolites that support growth, adaptation, morphogenesis, and
defence (Hmani et al. 2023; Lang et al. 2024). For example, several epiphytic isolates
produce enzymes such as amylase, lipase, and agarase that facilitate the degradation
of organic matter, as well as antimicrobial compounds that protect seaweed against
pathogens (Pinteus et al. 2021; Hmani et al. 2023). Bacterial species from the genus
97 Page 18 of 53                                             Aquaculture International (2026) 34:97


Paracoccus attached to Ulva are known to release regulatory molecules that stimulate
seaweed growth and morphogenesis (Hmani et al. 2023). Complementing this, func-
tional epiphytic bacteria on U. pinnatifida can enhance key host physiological processes
by augmenting carbon and nitrogen assimilation (Chen et al. 2025). Thus, companion
bacteria underpin seaweed life cycles by supplying essential nutrients, providing molec-
ular signals for environmental adaptation, and producing growth-promoting regulators.
In return, bacteria obtain nutrition from seaweed exudates and metabolic residues, con-
sistent with the enrichment of amino-acid and carbon transport genes in host-associated
bacteria (Pfister et al. 2025).
    However, this symbiosis is not invariably mutualistic; under certain conditions, epi-
phytic bacteria can become opportunists or pathogens (Saha and Weinberger 2019). In P.
yezoensis, for instance, harmful bacterial consortia proliferate at elevated temperatures and
can cause tissue degradation (Zeb et al. 2024). Thick biofilms on the thallus surface can
block light penetration required for photosynthesis, reduce nutrient availability, and hinder
gas exchange, thereby markedly impairing seaweed growth (Kaur et al. 2023; Senthil Rathi
et al. 2024). On contaminated thalli, biofilm formation is often dominated by Vibrio spp.
and Bacillus spp., which readily colonise the organic surface matrix (Pinteus et al. 2021).
Seaweed responses to biofilms are not uniform; some species deploy chemical and physical
defences that inhibit initial colonisation and biofilm maturation. For example, the Halyme-
nia releases allelopathic compounds that disrupt biofilm development and reduce microbial
attachment to the thallus surface (Abdul Malik et al. 2020). By contrast, Kappaphycus spp.
frequently experience biofilm attachment on the thallus. Although such biofilms do not
always trigger disease directly, their presence correlates with increased microbial growth
that can heighten host susceptibility (Kambey et al. 2021b). Consequently, biofilm control
is an operational priority in aquaculture systems, and routine cleaning of thalli and culture
substrates has been reported to effectively reduce biofilm accumulation and, in turn, the
risk of seaweed health disorders (Kambey et al. 2021b).
    The composition of bacterial communities adhering to seaweeds is strongly conditioned
by the environment, and pathogenic potential can shift accordingly (see Fig. 3). Bacteria
that are common in healthy individuals may become opportunistic pathogens when con-
ditions change. For example, bacteria and cyanobacteria that are native members of the
epiphytic microbiome on Pyropia spp. blades can turn opportunistic under certain environ-
mental regimes, such as increased seawater temperature (Kim et al. 2014; Zeb et al. 2024).
Exiguobacterium oxidotolerans XP-2, initially dominant on healthy S. japonica thalli,
was identified as an opportunistic pathogen that caused seaweed mortality in laboratory
assays, as demonstrated by fulfilment of Koch’s postulates (Ahmad et al. 2021). Similarly,
Pseudomonas isolates that occur naturally on healthy S. japonica thalli can induce disease
symptoms under controlled conditions, indicating that pathogenic behaviour emerges when
environmental factors and host status are permissive (Yang et al. 2023). Collectively, these
findings underscore a context-dependent boundary between bacterial commensalism and
pathogenicity in seaweeds and highlight the importance of farm-management practices that
prevent microbiome shifts toward disease-promoting states. Bacteria–seaweed associations
also exhibit marked host specificity. For instance, a particular strain of V. alginolyticus
(X-2) that naturally colonises S. japonica confers protection by reducing the risk of BD in
that seaweed (Ma et al. 2023). In contrast, other V. alginolyticus strains have been reported
as pathogens causing RD (locally named “Baotou”) in the agarophyte G. lemaneiformis
(Pang et al. 2024, 2025). These contrasting cases illustrate how environmental change and
host identity can shift bacterial roles from mutualistic to pathogenic, indicating that patho-
genicity risk should be assessed at the strain level rather than solely at the species level.

Parasites

Parasitism is an interaction in which a parasite benefits at the expense of its host, in this
case cultivated seaweeds. At the cellular level, parasitic colonisation is often accompa-
nied by the release of enzymes that degrade the cell wall and exploit structural polysac-
charides as nutrient sources, ultimately undermining tissue integrity and the seaweed’s
metabolic capacity (Jormalainen et al. 2001). As of 2025, accumulating empirical evi-
dence indicates that parasites are key determinants of seaweed health (Table 1) and
major drivers of productivity decline across several seaweed commodities, particularly
in East Asia. Among the most consistently reported groups are the oomycetes, with ris-
ing incidence in recent years across multiple production hubs (Zuccarello et al. 2024;
Friedlander and Critchley 2025b). Olpidiopsis pyropiae has been identified as a prin-
cipal pathogen in Pyropia aquaculture in South Korea, causing spots that damage thal-
lus blades and degrade the quality of both dried and fresh products (Kim et al. 2023;
Bae et al. 2024). Beyond Olpidiopsis, Pythium species such as P. porphyrae and P.
chondricola also infect Pyropia and contribute to outbreaks, including reports of RRD
and RSD that can precipitate harvest failures from local to regional scales (Badis et al.
2020a; Lee and Lee 2022; Bae et al. 2024). Although taxonomically related, Olpidiop-
sis and Pythium exhibit contrasting infection biology with direct management implica-
tions. Olpidiopsis is an obligate parasite that infects only living cells; its cycle involves
the release of zoospores from zoosporangia, and these zoospores remain viable in the
97 Page 20 of 53                                           Aquaculture International (2026) 34:97


environment for approximately 1 week, during which they must locate a new host to
continue the cycle. By contrast, Pythium can persist as a saprotroph on decaying sea-
weed tissue during the off-season, maintaining pathogen reservoirs even when primary
host populations decline (Park et al. 2025). These differences help explain variability
in field outbreak dynamics and necessitate tailored interventions. Oomycete infections
broadly disrupt tissue architecture and function, inducing morphological change, phys-
iological impairment, and reduced growth rates that culminate in losses of yield and
quality, with tangible economic impacts for farmers (Spagnuolo and Genovese 2024;
Kim et al. 2023).
   An ecological perspective further clarifies that risk does not originate solely within
farmed systems. Recent observations show that parasites and gall formation are also
common in wild seaweed populations, indicating that infected natural populations may
act as reservoirs or vectors transmitting pathogens to farms via currents, thallus frag-
ments, or biotic intermediaries. Reports from Chile and the Southeast Pacific document
the first cases of P. porphyrae infection in Pyropia in that region. At Gracilaria chil-
ensis farms where Pyropia is often considered a fouling pest on culture infrastructure,
outbreaks of P. porphyrae were followed by the collapse of Pyropia populations within
weeks, suggesting that pathogens can function as natural pest regulators in Gracilaria
polyculture, albeit with residual risk across commodities (Munoz et al. 2024). In South
Korea, Bangia fuscopurpurea, commonly viewed as a nuisance on Pyropia culture nets,
serves as an alternative host for P. porphyrae and Olpidiopsis, thereby expanding the
infection range and prolonging pathogen life cycles in farm environments. In P. yezoen-
sis, infections originating in Bangia can spread to newly developing Pyropia thalli,
forming an epidemiological bridge that heightens outbreak risk if unmanaged (Park
et al. 2025). Collectively, these findings underscore the need to map interspecific inter-
action networks within and around farming sites, because species once regarded as mere
nuisances may in fact operate as crucial links in pathogen transmission.

Viruses

The presence and dynamics of viruses in seaweed aquaculture are governed by interac-
tions between abiotic stressors and host susceptibility. Water pollution and other anthro-
pogenic pressures compromise seaweed physiological health and may facilitate viral
infection, while physical characteristics of the environment such as water movement and
wave exposure influence infection prevalence in particular species (Lovdal et al. 2021;
Martínez et al. 2024; Fig. 3). Climate warming is expected to increase the incidence
of viral disease, consistent with the role of viruses as major biotic agents of mortality
for many marine organisms, including seaweed (Benites and Alves-Lima 2022; Zhao
et al. 2024). To date, viral invasion has been reported in at least 54 seaweed species
(Schroeder and Mckeown 2021). The diversity of seaweed-infecting viruses can be
grouped broadly into non-pathogenic or even host-beneficial viruses, potential patho-
gens that may cause disease under specific conditions, and virulent pathogens that pose
substantial risks to seaweed aquaculture and global food security through damage to
hatcheries and farms (Mizutani et al. 2022; Murúa et al. 2023; Benites and Alves-Lima
2022). Despite growing inventories, our understanding of the full virosphere associated
with seaweeds remains limited, research on red seaweed viruses has progressed more
slowly than work on green seaweed viruses, and only a handful of seaweed viruses have
Aquaculture International (2026) 34:97                                        Page 21 of 53 97


been comprehensively characterised (Benites and Alves-Lima 2022; Martínez et al.
2024).
   Recent molecular evidence is beginning to resolve more nuanced causal relationships
among viruses, host status, and environmental context. Virome surveys and molecular
assays have identified numerous previously unrecognised viruses in seaweeds. For exam-
ple, in the green seaweed Ulva, several Circular rep-encoding single-stranded (CRESS)
DNA viruses and picorna-like viruses were highly abundant on bleached thalli but absent
or rare on healthy thalli (van der Loos et al. 2023). In the brown seaweed S. latissima,
phaeoviruses with double-stranded DNA were detected within host tissues (Dekker et al.
2024). In A. esculenta, both phaeoviruses and an unclassified RNA virus designated Aev-
NL1, related to ormycoviruses, were associated with the host (Dekker et al. 2024). Notably,
asymptomatic A. esculenta also harboured these viruses, indicating that viral presence does
not necessarily translate into disease phenotypes (Dekker et al. 2024). In Ulva, bleach-
ing appears to involve both bacteria and viruses, including picorna-like and CRESS-DNA
groups that proliferate in weakened or bacterially infected tissues, suggesting that co-infec-
tion may exacerbate disease progression. However, definitive causality still requires isola-
tion of pure viral cultures and controlled infection trials (van der Loos et al. 2023). More
direct aetiological evidence exists for Pyropia, in which a Pyropia-infecting virus has been
linked to GSD in cultivation systems, demonstrating that specific viruses can act as pri-
mary agents under conditions conducive to replication and transmission in seaweed hosts
(Kim et al. 2016). These observations underscore the need for further research focused on
viral isolation, experimental demonstration of infectivity in seaweeds, and the exploration
of interactions between viruses and co-occurring pathogens such as bacteria, together with
their integrated impacts on hosts.
   Importantly, not all seaweed-associated viruses are detrimental; some may be neutral
or beneficial. Recent work detected RNA viruses in S. latissima across both bleached and
apparently healthy individuals, with Slv-NL2 and Slv-NL3 present in all samples from
disease-affected sites regardless of bleaching status, consistent with latent or asympto-
matic infection (Dekker et al. 2024). Conversely, healthy A. esculenta contained dsDNA
phaeovirus Feldmannia irregularis virus and several RNA viruses that were absent from
diseased samples, implying that certain viruses can persist without causing pathology and
may even associate passively with their hosts (Dekker et al. 2024). In red seaweed, viruses
may adopt latent or lysogenic strategies, persisting across multiple host generations with-
out overt cellular damage, in contrast to lytic infections (Benites and Alves-Lima 2022).
Some persistent seaweed viruses are hypothesised to be mutualistic; for instance, members
of the family Mitoviridae (ssRNA) occur persistently in cultured P. yezoensis and appear
non-parasitic, with potential benefits to the host, although mechanisms remain unresolved
(Mizutani et al. 2022). Such findings raise the possibility that certain viruses function anal-
ogously to beneficial endosymbionts, for example by stabilising the microbiome or enhanc-
ing host stress tolerance (Mizutani et al. 2022). Recent advances highlight seaweed virol-
ogy as an increasingly vital field in aquaculture. Exploring viruses both as pathogens and
as potential allies requires a holistic research agenda. Such efforts are expected to yield
more effective disease control strategies and open opportunities to harness viruses for
enhancing resilience and productivity in cultivation (Mizutani et al. 2022).

Abiotic factors

Abiotic factors such as temperature, salinity, light intensity, nutrient availability, water
mass movement, and water quality modulate host physiological responses, alter the com-
position of the microbiome on seaweed surfaces and tissues, and ultimately increase sus-
ceptibility to opportunistic infection (Menaa et al. 2020; Veenhof et al. 2024; Zhang et al.
2024b). When environmental parameters exceed a species’ tolerance range, seaweeds expe-
rience physiological stress marked by reduced photosynthetic rates, biomass loss, weaken-
ing of cell wall integrity, and disrupted metabolism, thereby elevating the likelihood of
pathogen invasion (Hayashi et al. 2010; Tahiluddin and Terzi 2021; Martínez et al. 2024).
These conditions align with findings that seaweed diseases tend to emerge and spread more
rapidly under extreme environments, demonstrating a strong causal relationship between
physicochemical anomalies of seawater and disease dynamics (Veenhof et al. 2024). In par-
allel, Caley et al. (2025) show that warming increases community variability and elevates
the relative abundance of several putative pathogens. This relationship is also illustrated in
Table 1, which summarises information on the role of various abiotic factors in triggering
and exacerbating disease outbreaks. Likewise, Fig. 3 illustrates the differences in abiotic
conditions in relation to seaweed conditions.
    A close linkage between mechanism and field evidence is evident in Pyropia farming in
South Korea. Planting calendars have traditionally been arranged to exploit cold tempera-
tures from October to March in order to suppress RRD. The pathogen responsible for RRD,
P. porphyrae, releases zoospores with higher success when water temperature rises and
salinity declines, so winter months act as a natural brake on this disease cycle (Schroeder
et al. 2013; Kim et al. 2023). Progressive winter warming and greater unpredictability have
disrupted this brake, leading farmers not only to move to deeper waters but also to apply
clean freezing at around − 20 °C before the planting season. This strategy reduces risk, yet
it is energy and cost intensive and relies on synthetic materials that may increase pollution
burdens if waste is not properly managed (Kim et al. 2023; Sugumaran et al. 2022). In
China, Pyropia farmers have explored cultivation in deeper offshore waters as an adaptive
strategy to improve thermal stability and mitigate disease risks, including seedling rot (Liu
et al. 2025c). This approach underscores the importance of spatial planning as an environ-
mental adaptation instrument (Wang et al. 2025). Thermal sensitivity has also been docu-
mented for S. japonica in southern China, specifically in Fujian Province (a subtropical
region), where more than half of S. japonica production takes place. Rising temperatures
have been reported to reduce seed quality, increase disease incidence, and under extreme
conditions even prevent completion of the life cycle without human intervention during
summer months (Zhang et al. 2020; Hu et al. 2021; Cottier-Cook et al. 2022). A body of
evidence from cellular to field scales consistently indicates that abiotic variability is a pri-
mary driver of seaweed disease risk.
    Special attention should be directed to IID, given its high incidence reported globally,
particularly in seaweed aquaculture within tropical zones (Fig. 1). In intertidal farming
systems in shallow waters, tidal dynamics induce direct exposure to solar radiation and
fluctuations in temperature and salinity. Combined thermal and osmotic stress leads to
depigmentation and tissue dysfunction in seaweed, followed by colonisation by opportun-
istic microorganisms on seaweed surfaces and thalli (McHugh 2003). In addition, elevated
temperatures reduce the seaweed chemical defence capacity, increasing the likelihood of
dysbiosis (Kumar et al. 2020; Kopprio et al. 2021b; Tahiluddin and Terzi 2021; Hudson
et al. 2022; Rupert et al. 2023). Empirically, warmer waters are associated with higher
Aquaculture International (2026) 34:97                                       Page 23 of 53 97


prevalence of IID in eucheumatoids and gracilarioids, reflected in reduced yields and carra-
geenan quality (Munisamy et al. 2024; Friedlander and Critchley 2025a). In Zanzibar, IID
prevalence peaks in the hot dry season and declines markedly during the rainy season, indi-
cating a strong linkage with seasonal thermal dynamics (Largo et al. 2020). Field reports
from East Africa note sea surface temperatures exceeding 38 °C that trigger mass mortality
in farmed seaweeds, while in India, recurrent marine heatwaves have been associated with
IID outbreaks in K. alvarezii that disrupt the national carrageenan economy (Ndawala et al.
2021; Mantri et al. 2024; Dineshkumar et al. 2024; Innocentini et al. 2024). These data are
consistent with the view that thermal anomalies and salinity fluctuations are important con-
tributing factors associated with IID occurrence (see Table 1). This is supported by Atewe-
berhan et al. (2015) who emphasised that peak temperatures and high variability suppress
growth and compromise the health of K. alvarezii, so mitigation requires cooler scheduling
and careful site selection. The success of community-based projects depends on managing
ecological constraints through appropriate site choice and planting windows to maintain
optimal growing conditions.
    At the same time, historical and contemporary literature frequently reports the presence
of diverse bacterial assemblages in seaweed tissues exhibiting IID symptoms, particularly
members of the family Vibrionaceae and other marine Gram-negative groups. Isolates such
as Vibrio, Pseudomonas, Aeromonas, Pasteurella, Edwardsiella, Plesiomonas, and Chro-
mobacterium have been recovered from bleached thalli, giving rise to early hypotheses that
bacteria may act as causal agents transmitted through the water column (Aris and Labenua
2020; Astriana et al. 2024; Bessie et al. 2024; Tuhumury et al. 2024). However, research
outcomes remain highly variable across locations and seasons. Many identifications are
limited to the genus level using conventional culture-based approaches, and selective media
that favour Vibrio spp. introduce methodological bias. These constraints complicate cross-
study comparisons and limit consistent fulfilment of causality criteria required to substanti-
ate a primary bacterial aetiology (Azis et al. 2022; Ward et al. 2021).
    In this context, the accumulated evidence increasingly supports a pathobiome-based
interpretation of IID, in which disease emerges from interactions between host physiol-
ogy, microbial communities, and environmental stressors. Within this framework, abiotic
factors, particularly thermal anomalies, reduced salinity, and nutrient limitation, play a
central role in initiating physiological disturbance and microbial community shifts, while
opportunistic microorganisms act as secondary contributors that exacerbate tissue dam-
age rather than as sole aetiological agents (Ward et al. 2021). Field observations of mucus
accumulation on diseased thalli are consistent with this interpretation and are attributed to
the release of organic compounds, including carrageenan, following cell wall disintegra-
tion during bleaching events. These organic substrates subsequently provide a resource for
opportunistic bacteria, accelerating symptom development without constituting evidence
of a primary bacterial cause (Nasmia et al. 2014; Riyaz et al. 2021; Bessie et al. 2024).
    Further support for an environmental trigger paradigm is provided by recent field stud-
ies demonstrating correlations between IID prevalence and symptom intensity with dis-
solved ammonium concentrations and rainfall, but not with other measured parameters
such as temperature, salinity, pH, phosphate, nitrate, or nitrite (Tahiluddin and Terzi 2024).
These findings should be interpreted cautiously, as increased rainfall is known to reduce
salinity and enhance terrestrial runoff, thereby indirectly intensifying osmotic stress and
altering dissolved organic matter availability. Taken together, the current corpus of evi-
dence does not support attributing IID to specific bacterial taxa in the absence of environ-
mental triggers. Instead, it favours a multifactorial, holobiont-level disease model in which

environmentally mediated host stress sets the stage for opportunistic microbial proliferation
within a broader eco-physiological context.
   From a practical perspective, this conceptual framing has direct implications for disease
mitigation, positioning environmental stabilisation and context-sensitive risk management
as primary priorities in seaweed aquaculture. In tropical regions, farmers commonly adjust
cultivation calendars to avoid thermal extremes by suspending planting during periods of
elevated sea surface temperatures and resuming production with the onset of the rainy sea-
son. Sanitation practices, such as pruning infected segments, can reduce inoculum pressure
within farm units. However, indiscriminate disposal of infected fragments into surrounding
waters may facilitate wider dissemination of pathogens, underscoring the need for enforced
safe waste management procedures (Msuya 2020; Kambey et al. 2021b; Ward et al. 2021).
Relocating farms to deeper waters has also been proposed as a means to stabilise tempera-
ture and reduce solar radiation exposure (Wenno et al. 2015; Pang and Liu 2019), yet this
strategy is constrained by limited access, capital requirements, technical capacity, and the
need to initiate new production cycles. Moreover, relocation does not uniformly guarantee
reduced disease prevalence across ecological contexts. Indeed, Faisan et al. (2021) reported
a trend toward higher IID prevalence in deeper waters, although the difference was not
statistically significant, highlighting the importance of site-specific evaluation and continu-
ous environmental monitoring (de Jong Cleyndert et al. 2021). Social dimensions within
coastal communities, including gender roles, access to boats, and swimming ability, further
influence the feasibility of such interventions and must be considered to avoid exacerbating
participation inequalities (Ngajilo et al. 2025; Mwanyoka et al. 2025).
   Overall, the convergence of ecological evidence and field experience reinforces the
central role of abiotic drivers in shaping IID dynamics and indicates that effective mitiga-
tion should begin with environment informed risk management as the first line of defence.
This should be complemented by high-resolution holobiont monitoring and multifactorial
experiments that disentangle environmental triggers from bacterial cofactor roles. Such an
integrated approach provides a more robust scientific basis for designing location- and sea-
son-specific interventions while enhancing the resilience of seaweed production systems
under increasing climate variability.



Economic consequences and risks of diseases

Asian countries currently dominate global seaweed production, led by China, Indonesia,
South Korea, and the Philippines. This dominance rests on favourable environmental con-
ditions that include extensive coastlines, relatively low capital and resource requirements,
and minimal dependence on groundwater or freshwater, rendering aquaculture highly rel-
evant for coastal communities (Mariño et al. 2019; Mantri et al. 2024; Jaiswar et al. 2025).
This relevance is reflected in the high involvement of coastal households; for example,
more than 200,000 households in the Philippines and 267,000 in Indonesia rely on seaweed
farming for their livelihoods (Langford et al. 2021; Faisan and Hurtado 2024). Despite
these prospects, the sector faces serious biological pressures from disease outbreaks that
directly suppress economic performance.
   In tropical regions, IID is widely recognised as the most frequent disease affecting sea-
weed aquaculture, and it contributes to production declines. Its impact is evident in produc-
tion trends in Africa. Total output dominated by eucheumatoid species, namely the genera
Kappaphycus and Eucheuma, fell from 178,000 tonnes in 2015 to 81,000 tonnes in 2021
Aquaculture International (2026) 34:97                                       Page 25 of 53 97


(UNCTAD 2024). In Tanzania, production in 2020 was approximately 11,000 tonnes with
a value of about USD 2 million, down from 16,000 tonnes valued at around USD 4 mil-
lion in 2015. One driver of this decline is IID, which can be exacerbated by contempo-
rary climate change (Kunzmann et al. 2023). Epidemiologically, IID can reach very high
prevalence, up to 99% in eucheumatoid farms (Largo et al. 2020), and even E. denticula-
tum, often regarded as more tolerant in earlier reports, has been affected (Pang et al. 2015;
Ndawala et al. 2022). Unsurprisingly, the disease is acknowledged as a serious threat to the
sustainability of farming in Tanzania (Msuya et al. 2022). Similar patterns appear across
tropical Asia. In the Philippines, IID prevalence has been reported at 54% in Tawi-Tawi
(Tahiluddin and Terzi 2024) and up to 100% in Mindanao (Faisan et al. 2021). Lower val-
ues for Kappaphycus in Eastern Samar likely reflect observational bias due to daily thallus
cleaning by farmers and may not capture true infection intensity in the field (Yap-Dejeto
et al. 2021). In Malaysia, particularly Sabah as a production hub, output declined by 1.7%
from 182,000 tonnes in 2020 to 178,800 tonnes in 2021, and IID outbreaks have been indi-
cated as one of the contributors to this reduction (DoF 2021; Kambey et al. 2021b). Finan-
cially, IID is identified as the principal disease in Kappaphycus and has been linked to local
losses of approximately USD 17,300 to 18,500 per farm in Indonesia, as well as estimated
annual losses of about USD 100 million in the Philippines (Cottier-Cook et al. 2016; Ward
et al. 2020, 2021). Although national production in Indonesia and the Philippines remains
high, disease pressure can erode profit margins, increase raw material supply volatility, and
impede the attainment of growth targets for the sector. At policy and research levels, there
is a need to harmonise surveillance protocols such as case definitions, severity scales, and
loss reporting to conduct multi-season field trials across sites and to undertake microeco-
nomic analyses at the farm unit level so that proposed interventions can be evaluated for
both biological effectiveness and financial feasibility. Consequently, even though aggre-
gate output in Indonesia and the Philippines remains substantial, trends in IID occurrence
and its sensitivity to environmental change indicate that, without strengthened surveillance,
standardised metrics, and adaptive farming practices, Kappaphycus production systems in
tropical Asia are vulnerable to long-term productivity declines and supply fluctuations.
    Losses arise not only in harvested quantity but also in carrageenan quality. Multiple
studies show that carrageenan yield and gel strength in K. alvarezii and E. denticulatum
are consistently higher in healthy biomass than in infected samples (Mendoza et al. 2002;
Rantetondok and Latama 2017), consistent with findings that carrageenan quality declines
in diseased seaweeds (Riyaz et al. 2021; Ndawala et al. 2022). Mechanistically, this qual-
ity degradation can be explained by two main processes. First, damage to the thallus sur-
face, which is a key site for the synthesis and accumulation of cell wall polysaccharides,
disrupts carrageenan biosynthesis (Ndawala et al. 2022). Second, enzymatic hydrolysis by
pathogen-produced carrageenases cleaves carrageenan chains into lower molecular mass
fragments, reducing gel strength and increasing losses during precipitation and extrac-
tion (Riyaz et al. 2022). As a consequence, infected biomass commands lower prices due
to deteriorating hydrocolloid quality, which in turn can depress farm gate prices, shrink
coastal household incomes, and heighten local economic instability. At the industry scale,
chronic production disruptions can diminish market confidence and undermine the conti-
nuity of the global carrageenan supply chain. If outbreaks persist without effective inter-
vention, some farming areas risk enterprise collapse (Khan et al. 2024). This context is
particularly salient for major producers such as Indonesia, which has long supplied carra-
geenan raw materials to China as a global processing hub for the food, pharmaceutical, and
biotechnological applications (Zhang et al. 2024a).

    Disease pressure also compels farmers to adjust production strategies. A common
response to increasing infection intensity is the temporary suspension of farming activities.
This response should be understood not merely as a short-term operational adjustment,
but rather as a consequence of structural constraints within coastal production systems,
where few alternative commodities are simultaneously viable from both biophysical and
economic perspectives. Ecologically, seaweeds require relatively narrow ranges of environ-
mental conditions, particularly with respect to temperature and salinity, which substantially
limit the spectrum of species that can be cultivated at a given site. For example, S. japonica
is a cold-water seaweed, whereas K. alvarezii requires tropical environments. Switching
species without altering environmental parameters is therefore often impractical or even
impossible. These biological constraints are reinforced by market barriers. Well established
market integration for carrageenan producing commodities keeps many farmers focused on
Eucheuma spp., making diversification into other species risky in terms of market accept-
ance and profitability (Langford et al. 2022). Although E. denticulatum shows greater tol-
erance to IID and has a faster growth cycle of approximately 10 to 15 days compared with
about 45 days in K. alvarezii (van Oort et al. 2025), typically fetches lower prices, around
10 to 50 PHP per kg in the Philippines and approximately IDR 6000 to 7500 per kg dry
weight in East Nusa Tenggara, Indonesia, whereas K. alvarezii reaches 90 to 140 PHP per
kg, about USD 0.69 to 1.09 per kg dry weight in India, and roughly IDR 20,000 to 22,000
per kg dry weight in Indonesia (Valderrama et al. 2015; Mantri et al. 2022; Tahiluddin
et al. 2023; Tahiluddin and Roleda 2025b; Bessie et al. 2025). This price disparity makes
diversification less attractive because profit margins are insufficient to sustain livelihoods,
particularly for low-income coastal households. In several locations such as Zanzibar,
where the majority of farmers are women, limited access to capital, market information,
and trading networks further constrains adaptive options (Matoju et al. 2022). These lines
of evidence indicate that disease in cultured seaweed species generates systemic economic
impacts that not only erode local farm incomes but also threaten supply stability for down-
stream industries that rely on seaweed as a raw material.
    Evidence from China and South Korea shows that both countries have suffered economic
losses arising from disease outbreaks in seaweed cultivation. In Pyropia, disease outbreaks
in China can cause regional yield losses of 25 to 30% (Gachon et al. 2010). Among these
diseases, RRD is one of the most destructive, with substantial impacts on both yields and
economic returns. For instance, yields reportedly declined by 84% in Fuding City, Fujian
Province, China (Lai 2009). In January 2018, P. yezoensis declines were estimated at 50%,
covering more than 7000 hectares in Haizhou Bay, Jiangsu Province, China (Yan et al.
2019). Since 2017, farmers in the Jiangsu production hub have also reported bleaching,
with estimated losses of around USD 410 million in 2021, while pathogen identification
was published only subsequently (BoF and NFTEC 2022; Liu et al. 2025b). Impacts extend
to other commodities. National harvest volumes of G. lemaneiformis reportedly fell by at
least 40% in 2023 compared with the previous year due to outbreaks of RD (Pang et al.
2024). Meanwhile, losses of 25 to 30% of regional harvest volumes of S. japonica in China
have been attributed to diseases and pests as reported earlier (Wang et al. 2014), and these
conditions are summarised in Table 1 and Fig. 1. Subsequently, S. japonica was affected by
WSD with reductions in sporophyte length of up to 42% per day during 2017 to 2018, and
the trend continued in 2021 when various S. japonica diseases were still reported along the
Rongcheng coast of Shandong Province, the main production centre, causing large eco-
nomic losses. The scale and persistence of these events were reported to be unprecedented
in the past five decades (Wang et al. 2021; Zheng et al. 2025).
Aquaculture International (2026) 34:97                                        Page 27 of 53 97


   In South Korea, disease incidence is estimated to reduce Pyropia yields, including
through RRD. Park et al. (2000) and Kim et al. (2014) documented declines in yield and
prices of up to 15 to 20% and 20 to 30% in Pyropia farming areas in South Korea, respec-
tively. Although affected seaweeds can still be harvested and marketed, lower quality and
yields impose substantial negative economic impacts (Kim et al. 2014). In some farming
seasons, GSD has caused substantial economic losses. For example, in 2012 to 2013, a
GSD outbreak in the Seocheon sea farm in South Korea resulted in losses of about USD
1.1 million, comparable to losses from OD of USD 1.6 million (Kim et al. 2014). For U.
pinnatifida, traditional practice generally spans October to April, but to avoid PD, farmers
have shortened the farming period to December to February. This shorter harvest window
reduces production per farm area. Park et al. (2008) showed that maximum growth in U.
pinnatifida occurs between March and April and estimated that early harvesting to evade
PD could reduce total biomass by roughly one third, equivalent to about 5 to 8 tonnes wet
weight per hectare. The persistence of these diseases not only depresses annual yields
but also undermines long-term productivity and the bioeconomic stability of aquaculture
through supply uncertainty, rising mitigation costs, and risks of declining product quality.
This evidence underscores that the economic burden of seaweed diseases is now apparent
across all producing nations.



Key aspects of disease management in seaweed cultivation

Biosecurity measures

The presence of diverse pathogens whose pressure is increasing due to climate change,
together with abiotic factors such as water movement and light intensity, has the potential
to amplify disease risk in seaweed aquaculture (Ward et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2024b). This
situation underscores the urgency of strengthening biosecurity protocols as the first line
of defence. Internationally, biosecurity is recognised as a key approach to minimise the
introduction and spread of disease within the aquaculture industry. To be effective, imple-
mentation requires interdisciplinary governance and regulatory mechanisms at national and
international levels (Campbell et al. 2020), particularly in the context of increasingly inten-
sive cross-border trade (Bray et al. 2024). At the national level, strategies must be tailored
to local challenges while being aligned with the development of progressive management
pathways for the seaweed sector (Cottier-Cook et al. 2022). Clear, simple, and integrated
strategies are expected to raise vigilance, promote the adoption of sustainable manage-
ment systems, and streamline research and policy development by reducing cross-sector
redundancies (Hulme 2021). This urgency is sharpened by the growing interconnectedness
between aquaculture production and global trade flows.
    In practice, current biosecurity measures focus on prevention, treatment, and mitigation
of disease, while truly effective control remains a central challenge (Bray et al. 2024). This
is understandable because seaweed is cultivated in open waters that are difficult to control,
unlike terrestrial crops or aquatic animals in ponds that grow in more controlled environ-
ments. Nevertheless, field evidence shows that simple interventions can materially reduce
risk. A case study in Sabah, Malaysia, showed that the use of healthy propagules and the
cleaning of thalli and ropes can reduce disease spread in seaweed farms (Kambey et al.
2021b). Evaluation of biosecurity implementation should be conducted through systematic
analysis of policy frameworks, regulations, and national standards. In Indonesia, identified
97 Page 28 of 53                                             Aquaculture International (2026) 34:97


barriers include a limited range of biosecurity approaches, low uptake of modern technolo-
gies, and inadequate prevention and treatment procedures (Kambey et al. 2020). A relevant
example comes from farming experiments in South Sulawesi, a major seaweed production
hub in Indonesia (Alam et al. 2025), which documented the reuse of biomass previously
exposed to IID after only separating it from infected thalli, washing it, and then replant-
ing it in the next cycle (van Oort et al. 2025). This practice increases the likelihood of
disease persistence and transmission into subsequent production cycles and reflects weak
ecology and technology-based mitigation at the farm level. Conversely, correct application
of biosecurity principles including the removal of infected seaweeds in the field has been
shown to improve seaweed health and reduce disease impacts by up to 83% in treated plots
(Sondak et al. 2025). In practice, removal of thalli is often conducted by cutting affected
segments and allowing apparently unaffected portions to regenerate and regrow, although
overall productivity declines (Hurtado et al. 2019).
   These findings provide an evidence-based foundation that biosecurity plays a central
role in preventing and mitigating seaweed diseases while supporting improvements in both
quantity and quality of production. Accordingly, investment in farm-level biosecurity prac-
tices can generate economic gains through reduced outbreak losses and increased yields. In
addition, more detailed and coherent biosecurity policies are needed to support the growth
of the seaweed industry and deliver global benefits through cross-border policy integra-
tion. Knowledge, attitudes, and practices surveys, or KAP, can be used to design effective
policies and management strategies to address biosecurity risks in the seaweed aquaculture
sector. This method, which has been applied in Malaysia, is expected to improve industry
governance and support sustained productivity (Kambey et al. 2021a).
   Although aquaculture has long been practiced, the sector is also a major pathway for
the spread of alien species in aquatic ecosystems. To date, there is no dedicated health
code for aquatic plants nor a primary international mechanism designed to control diseases
associated with the trade of seaweed-based organisms and aquaculture products. In other
words, there are no globally recognised cross-national standards for reporting, diagnosis,
or control of seaweed disease outbreaks. This gap causes many outbreaks to escape global
attention, to be managed locally, and often to remain poorly documented (Cottier-Cook
et al. 2021). Concerns intensify when lesser-known pathogens are inadvertently introduced
through agricultural and horticultural trade or through the use of seedstock sourced from
outside the country. In 2005, in an effort to rescue the seaweed farming industry in Kudat,
Sabah, Malaysia, which was severely affected by IID, seedlings were imported from south-
ern Philippines. Rather than solving the problem, this action exacerbated the situation by
introducing pathogens that triggered more severe outbreaks such as secondary infections
and thallus breakage due to inadequate monitoring and quarantine (Vairappan et al. 2008).
Similar concerns were raised by Badis et al. (2020b), who concluded that international
movement of seaweed seed without monitoring or biosecurity regulation could form the
basis of a serious ecological and economic crisis in marine environments.
   This contrasts with the aquatic animal sector, which is regulated by the Aquatic ani-
mal health code, developed by the World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH, for-
merly the Office International des Epizooties). This code is part of the legal framework
of the World Trade Organization (WTO) and governs measures for the prevention, early
detection, reporting, notification, and control of pathogens in aquatic animals, aimed at
preventing their spread through international trade. The consequences of regulatory gaps
in the seaweed sector are illustrated by documented cases from Indonesia, Tanzania, and
the Philippines, as well as reports of unauthorised cultivation of S. japonica in southern
Chile, a species that is non-native to the region. In this case, farming activities reportedly
Aquaculture International (2026) 34:97                                         Page 29 of 53 97


occurred outside established approval processes for the introduction and cultivation of
alien seaweeds, highlighting weaknesses in biosecurity governance related to species trans-
location and bioinvasion risk (Camus et al. 2022). These lessons are particularly pertinent
for jurisdictions assessing the development of aquaculture based on new or expanded uses
of seaweed species. For example, while Hormosira banksii is a common intertidal sea-
weed naturally distributed throughout temperate New Zealand waters, any proposal to scale
up its utilisation for aquaculture purposes still necessitates robust biosecurity frameworks
(Loy and Jeffs 2025). Priorities include precautionary frameworks through pre border risk
assessment, quarantine procedures and certification of seed sources, and ecological and
genetic monitoring. Accordingly, clearer and more comprehensive regulations at national
and international levels are urgently needed. Collaboration through a One Biosecurity
approach has the potential to integrate fragmented frameworks and to better account for
cross sector risks and stakeholder needs (Bray et al. 2024).
   To address these gaps, FAO, in collaboration with aquaculture experts, developed the
Progressive Management Pathway for Improving Aquaculture Biosecurity for Seaweed
(PMP/AB-Seaweed), a framework specifically designed to address biosecurity challenges
in seaweed aquaculture (Cottier-Cook et al. 2022). This pathway emphasises the formation
of cross-sector task forces spanning public and private stakeholders, the development of
a National Strategy for Seaweed Health Management, the establishment of standardised
diagnostic, reporting, and response protocols, and the integration of seaweed health gov-
ernance into national fisheries or agricultural policies. Collectively, these components aim
to operationalise a One Health approach. Although by 2025 seaweed-specific international
standards remain under finalisation, this initiative represents an important step toward har-
monised disease management at the global level. Strengthening the scientific basis remains
urgent, including the clear establishment of disease aetiology and the further development
of diagnostic and therapeutic techniques that are still at an early stage, so that policy is not
formulated without adequate evidential support (Cottier-Cook et al. 2022). Insights from
the holobiont perspective indicate that managing beneficial microbiota can enhance resil-
ience (Kambey et al. 2021b). Looking ahead, probiotics, breeding, and genetic approaches
for disease-resistant varieties have the potential to complement conventional biosecurity.

The use of inorganic materials

The application of inorganic agents in seaweed aquaculture has strengthened in recent
years. This trend has been largely driven by the need to enhance productivity through
accelerated biomass accumulation and shortened harvest cycles (Roleda et al. 2025). In
line with this objective, nutrient enrichment using inorganic fertilisers such as NPK has
been employed to stimulate growth, improve tolerance to nutrient stress, and suppress the
incidence of IID in eucheumatoid seaweeds (Luhan et al. 2015; Tahiluddin and Terzi 2024;
Muyong and Tahiluddin 2024). Field evidence has strengthened the case for this approach.
Muyong and Tahiluddin (2024) demonstrated that ammonium and phosphate supplemen-
tation in K. alvarezii provided complete protection against IID across multiple cultiva-
tion methods tested in Tawi-Tawi, Southern Philippines. The practice was subsequently
extended to other species, particularly K. striatus, which has been empirically reported to
be more susceptible to IID than K. alvarezii and E. denticulatum (Tahiluddin and Eldani-
Tahiluddin 2024). Collectively, these findings indicate that inorganic interventions can
serve as a critical component of seaweed health management when applied appropriately
and under controlled conditions.
97 Page 30 of 53                                             Aquaculture International (2026) 34:97


    However, these technical benefits are accompanied by ecological, economic, and prod-
uct-quality considerations that require careful attention. From an environmental perspec-
tive, the use of synthetic inputs may stimulate eutrophication, alter the balance of coastal
microbiomes, and increase pollutant loads in and around aquaculture waters (Liu et al.
2020; Lähteenmäki-Uutela et al. 2021; Mancuso et al. 2023). From a production stand-
point, although inorganic fertilisers can accelerate eucheumatoid growth, they frequently
encourage premature harvesting before tissues reach full maturity. This can reduce gel
strength and carrageenan quality, ultimately lowering yield and product value (Roleda et al.
2025; Abduraup et al. 2025).
    The economic implications are equally significant. Dependence on commercial inor-
ganic fertilisers is incompatible with international organic certification standards, thereby
limiting access to premium markets and potentially weakening the competitiveness of
high-value seaweed products (Gómez and Lähteenmäki-Uutela 2021). Across the value
chain, these effects extend from primary production to downstream marketing. The use of
inorganic nutrient enrichment can disqualify seaweed biomass and its derivatives, includ-
ing carrageenan, from organic certification—particularly within food and feed produc-
tion chains (Tahiluddin and Roleda 2025a). Consequently, upstream production decisions
directly shape downstream market positioning.
    These implications are particularly relevant for major eucheumatoid-producing coun-
tries such as the Philippines, especially the provinces of Tawi-Tawi and Sulu, where inor-
ganic nutrient supplementation remains in cultivation (Tahiluddin et al. 2023). These
regions must reassess the balance between productivity-driven strategies and compliance
with target market standards. Recent literature advocates a transition toward more sustain-
able approaches through the integration of strict biosecurity, improved seedstock quality,
and the use of biofertilisers aligned with organic guidelines as alternatives to synthetic
inputs (Kambey et al. 2021b; Liu et al. 2024b; Alam et al. 2025). Achieving this tran-
sition will require coordinated policy support, sustained research investment, and farmer
extension programmes. The overarching goal is to develop farming systems that remain
productive, meet certification requirements, safeguard the integrity of coastal ecosystems,
and maintain access to high-value markets. Such an integrated approach enables effective
disease control without compromising organic legitimacy or the economic value of sea-
weed products.



Genetic diversification

Genetic diversification is the foundation of population genetics approaches to enhance
productivity, quality, and disease resistance in seaweed aquaculture. In many major sea-
weed commodities, particularly Kappaphycus and Eucheuma, the predominance of vegeta-
tive propagation, limited domestication, and the reuse of harvested biomass as seedstock
have produced farmed populations with low genetic diversity, a condition associated with
reduced growth rates, suboptimal morphology such as thinning thallus branching, and
heightened susceptibility to pathogens, as reported by Sundarraj et al. (2025) and Gonzaga
et al. (2025) on eucheumenoid populations in tropical regions. From a population genetics
perspective, prolonged cloning reduces effective population size, which in turn diminishes
genetic diversity or allelic richness and narrows the portfolio of genetic material available
to future generations and breeding efforts (Lim et al. 2017; Thomson 2021).
Aquaculture International (2026) 34:97                                       Page 31 of 53 97


    Efforts to broaden the genetic base through selection of local varieties and interstrain
crosses are logical first steps, yet the complexity of disease pressure and environmental
variability demands more targeted strategies grounded in population data. Monitoring
parameters such as heterozygosity, allelic richness, and among population differentiation
provides a diversity map that can be translated into seed decisions (Van Rossum and Hardy
2022), for example complementary crossing pairs, multi-site seed sources, and mosaic
planting across genotypes to disrupt pathogen transmission. In Malaysia, the discovery of
new haplotypes in wild Kappaphycus populations reveals untapped reservoirs of adaptive
alleles that can be introduced into farmed populations to strengthen long-term resilience
(Tan et al. 2022). This approach underscores the integration of population genetic surveys,
germplasm management, and breeding programmes so that adaptive alleles flow into cul-
ture stocks in a measured way.
    Compelling applications come from heteromorphic life cycle species at temperate lati-
tudes. Hybridisation programmes for S. japonica and U. pinnatifida in China and South
Korea leverage the gametophyte phase to produce F1 cultivars through controlled crosses.
Goecke et al. (2020) reported Saccharina hybrids with greater thallus size and wet weight
than parental lines, indicating heterosis relevant for productivity. Intraspecific and inter-
specific hybridisation can also combine adaptive alleles that enhance tolerance to thermal
stress, a key trait under ocean warming. Downstream, farmer concerns in Dongtou Dis-
trict, China, about declining performance of older varieties that have become increasingly
disease prone highlight the urgency of genetic renewal of culture stocks through planned
crossing schemes (Liu et al. 2025a). In line with this, South Korea has developed P.
yezoensis Gold 1, which shows high resilience to RRD and has been widely commercial-
ised, underscoring the value of locally based domestication and breeding to sustain high-
value commodities (Hyun et al. 2023).
    Advances in biotechnology expand the scope of intervention without departing from
population genetic principles. Marker-assisted breeding and, where available, genomic
selection can accelerate the identification and introgression of resistance alleles into supe-
rior genetic backgrounds. Recent evidence shows selectively developed lines that express
stronger immune responses to pathogen infection than conventional varieties, reinforcing
the value of data-driven targeted breeding (Zhuang et al. 2024). At the same time, spe-
cific crosses that exhibit heterosis not only increase growth rate but also broaden tolerance
thresholds to temperature fluctuations, which indirectly reduces vulnerability to opportun-
istic diseases triggered by environmental stress (Song et al. 2024). Integrating informa-
tion on genetic diversity, crossing design, and phenotypic evaluation of resistance therefore
yields breeding pipelines that are more predictive and efficient.
    Exploration of wild germplasm remains a pillar for adding variation, especially when
local cultivars show narrowing diversity. Findings from Malaysia confirm limited diversity
in local cultivars and the strategic value of prospecting wild populations as sources of new
adaptive alleles for Kappaphycus spp. and E. denticulatum that confer tolerance to high
temperature and pathogen infection (Tan et al. 2022; Yahya et al. 2024). Even so, recent
literature cautions that translocation of genetic material across locations or species must
be managed within strict biosecurity frameworks. Risks of genetic pollution and the emer-
gence of novel pathogen strains should be anticipated through genetic screening, multi-site
trials, and ecological impact assessment prior to varietal release, as highlighted by recent
ecological and policy reviews (Spillias et al. 2022; Crisostomo and Roleda 2024; Roleda
et al. 2024; Ngwaerobo et al. 2025).
    Overall, population genetics provides an operational set of concepts and metrics to
guide improvements in disease resistance in cultivated seaweeds. Mapping population
97 Page 32 of 53                                             Aquaculture International (2026) 34:97


structure and connectivity, quantifying genetic variation with molecular markers, and man-
aging gene flow between wild and farmed stocks enable the design of seed systems with
substantial genetic diversity. When combined with mosaic planting across genotypes, rota-
tion of seed sources, and cross-environment resistance evaluation, this approach not only
enhances agronomic performance but also slows pathogen adaptation. Looking ahead,
research should integrate regional population genetic surveys, resistance marker panels,
and standardised trials so that domestication yields cultivars that are robust and competi-
tive. In line with this trajectory, Gacura et al. (2026) reported new K. alvarezii haplotypes
that grow rapidly and are less susceptible to IID, which are now under multi-location test-
ing including in Bohol, Philippines.

Effectiveness of biostimulants

Biostimulants are defined as active compounds that are non-nutritional and non-pesti-
cide which modulate physiological processes in plants and offer substantial potential
to enhance growth while strengthening tolerance to environmental stress. In recent
years, their application in seaweed aquaculture has attracted global research attention
as a sustainable strategy to increase productivity and resilience to disease. One widely
studied biostimulant is the extract derived from the brown seaweed Ascophyllum nodo-
sum, which has shown notable efficacy in supporting large scale seedling production to
address bottlenecks in seedstock supply. Compared with liquid extracts such as those
from A. nodosum (Borlongan et al. 2024), Ascophyllum marine plant extract powder or
AMPEP is more widely used and has demonstrated superior performance in optimising
seaweed growth even under suboptimal environmental conditions (Hurtado et al. 2024).
For example, application of AMPEP to S. latissima significantly accelerates growth
rates at elevated temperatures that typically limit production (Hurtado and Critchley
2020), underscoring its potential to mitigate climate related constraints in seaweed farm-
ing. However, biological responses to AMPEP are species specific and conditioned by
environmental parameters. A study by Shin et al. (2024) showed that although AMPEP
did not increase thermal tolerance in P. yezoensis, it triggered reproduction through the
release of archeospores at low temperatures. These findings encourage exploration of
alternative biostimulants such as Sargassum horneri extracts that may support thermal
resilience in particular species (Han et al. 2023). In addition, Zainuddin (et al. 2019)
reported that dipping seedstock of seaweeds such as G. verrucosa in Caulerpa extracts
prior to pond culture is a promising step that warrants further testing to reduce damage
from IID in seaweed aquaculture. This aspect merits closer examination in relation to
infection dynamics since thermal stress at high temperature can heighten susceptibility
to opportunistic pathogens as also noted by Zainuddin et al. (2019).
   Beyond their role in promoting growth and tolerance to environmental pressures,
AMPEP has also proven effective as a prophylactic agent against disease. Significant
reductions in IID incidence have been observed in K. alvarezii treated with AMPEP
solution during the summer period (Munisamy et al. 2023; Capacio et al. 2024). This
effectiveness is consistent with evidence that AMPEP can activate natural defence
pathways in the red seaweed K. alvarezii against pathogens and environmental change
(Loureiro et al. 2012). From an economic standpoint, AMPEP is affordable for small-
holder farmers in developing countries. In India, for example, an additional production
cost of about USD 0.066 per kg of dry Kappaphycus in 2021 was associated with a
yield increase of 16.66% following AMPEP application (Shanmugam and Seth 2018;
Aquaculture International (2026) 34:97                                       Page 33 of 53 97


Munisamy et al. 2023). Nevertheless, inconsistency among batches, likely due to varia-
tion in chemical composition or extraction methods, can reduce effectiveness and there-
fore calls for standardisation of production processes (Hurtado et al. 2024).
   Alongside seaweed extract-based biostimulants such as AMPEP and liquid A. nodo-
sum products, functional microbe-based approaches also show considerable promise for
enhancing disease resistance. In China, probiotic P. piscicida strains (P3 and P6), espe-
cially when combined with short-term drying, effectively suppressed the development
of P. porphyrae, the pathogen that causes RRD in Pyropia, without damaging thalli
(Weng et al. 2024). A holobiont-oriented approach that leverages symbiotic interactions
between seaweeds and associated microbes further reinforces this concept. The use of V.
alginolyticus strain X 2 reduced bleaching in S. japonica and supports the development
of seaweed beneficial microorganism frameworks for cultivation (Ma et al. 2023). These
findings indicate the potential role of combining probiotics and biostimulants in sup-
porting more resilient and environmentally sustainable farming practices.
   Although biostimulants hold substantial promise, their effectiveness is shaped by envi-
ronmental factors such as temperature, salinity, and season. Optimal implementation there-
fore requires tuning of applications to local conditions. Large-scale deployment also faces
challenges that include product inconsistency, limited long-term datasets, and potential
ecological effects such as shifts in marine microbiomes, which require systematic evalu-
ation through multi-site field trials and extended monitoring (Brakel et al. 2021). As envi-
ronmentally friendly alternatives to synthetic inputs, biostimulants align with modern bios-
ecurity principles and contribute to the sustainable transformation of global aquaculture
(Jaiswar et al. 2025).

Application of co‑culture

Co-culture of seaweeds with other aquatic organisms has emerged as an innovative
approach in sustainable aquaculture with the aim of establishing symbiotic relationships
that support ecosystem stability and multi-species productivity. This approach mimics nat-
ural processes in coastal systems in which nutrient wastes generated by animals are utilised
by seaweeds, while the presence of seaweeds helps stabilise water quality through nutrient
uptake and oxygen production (Zhang et al. 2025). Although its ecological potential is rec-
ognised, much of the earlier literature has focused on positive effects for companion organ-
isms such as shrimp, crabs, fish, and sea cucumbers, whereas impacts on the physiology
and health of farmed seaweeds remain relatively overlooked (Pintado et al. 2023; Anh et al.
2024). For example, integrating Penaeus indicus with Gracilaria tenuistipitata increased
shrimp growth rate and immune responses (Das et al. 2022), and co-culture of Ulva per-
tusa and Gracilariopsis with scallops improved water quality, suppressed proliferation of
Vibrio spp., and strengthened bivalve immunity, although evidence on direct implications
for seaweed health is still limited (Zhang et al. 2025). Recent work shows that co-culture of
Gracilariopsis with Penaeus monodon did not alter agar content or gel strength and even
increased the specific growth rate of seaweeds, yet health parameters should be evaluated
explicitly so that growth health trade-offs can be assessed accurately and productivity gains
remain aligned with sustainable farming (Nauta et al. 2025).
   Co-culture provides a range of ecosystem services relevant to disease control, including
pathogen filtration, nutrient regulation, oxygenation, and microbial competition, which col-
lectively enhance biosecurity and system resilience (Zhang et al. 2025; Zaat et al. 2025).
In an intraspecific context, coexistence of S. horneri with G. lemaneiformis stimulates
97 Page 34 of 53                                             Aquaculture International (2026) 34:97


seaweed growth while maintaining a balanced biomass ratio that supports community sta-
bility (Song et al. 2022). Co-culture among seaweeds such as K. alvarezii, K. striatum,
and E. denticulatum has also been reported to reduce the incidence of IID compared with
homogeneous monoculture systems (Pang et al. 2015; Bessie et al. 2024). In pond-based
systems, combining G. verrucosa and C. racemosa shows potential to lessen damage from
IID and warrants further testing at operational scale to confirm consistency of benefits
across sites (Zainuddin et al. 2019). Taken together, these findings indicate that species
diversification in co-culture can create a mini ecosystem that is more robust to external
disturbance.
    Even so, co-culture is not risk free for seaweed health. Higher prevalence of IID in Kap-
paphycus within integrated multi-trophic aquaculture alongside sea cucumbers and oysters
indicates that combined thermal stress and nutrient deficiency can trigger host vulnerabil-
ity when environmental management is inadequate (Bernard et al. 2024). Physico-chemical
conditions such as temperature and nutrient availability are therefore critical to co-culture
success. The presence of non-target species can also act as pathogen reservoirs. Bangia
fuscopurpurea, which can coexist with farmed seaweeds, has been identified as a potential
reservoir of oomycetes that cause disease in P. yezoensis, implying that non-target species
may worsen disease dynamics even if they are not a direct component of the co-culture
system (Park et al. 2025). Furthermore, co-culture of G. lemaneiformis with S. japonica
has been reported to increase the risk of pathogen spread unless supported by strict risk
management at operational and biosecurity levels (Pang et al. 2025).
    Several mitigation strategies have been proposed to minimise these risks. In Europe,
probiotic colonisation of Ulva by Phaeobacter in co-culture with fish produced anti patho-
genic compounds that significantly reduced disease incidence, with effectiveness contin-
gent on microenvironment management including light cycle regimes to stabilise microbial
communities (Del Olmo et al. 2025). In parallel, selecting physiologically compatible spe-
cies is essential to suppress infection risk, so community design should consider nutritional
requirements, temperature tolerance, and host microbiome dynamics (Zaat et al. 2025). In
the context of climate adaptation, Ulva compressa can facilitate the growth of Ulva lacinu-
lata, which is sensitive to temperature fluctuation, indicating that intraspecific co-culture
can be a viable strategy to enhance thermal resilience in seaweeds (Bermejo et al. 2023).
    Overall, the available evidence indicates that co-culture can become a future farming
model that is productive, environmentally friendly, and resilient to climate variability and
disease pressure. Successful implementation requires science-based design, careful species
selection, and stringent pathogen control and biosecurity protocols. Continued research
is needed to refine this approach and to ensure that ecological gains and productivity
improvements are achieved without compromising the health of farmed seaweeds. Once
the scientific and operational foundations are established, the next priority is to promote
commercial scale adoption of polyculture, noting that farmers, particularly in develop-
ing countries such as Indonesia, require support to adopt co-culture practices, including
strengthened bargaining positions with intermediaries and access to finance that enables
initial investment (Azhari et al. 2026).

Multisectoral collaboration

Diseases affecting farmed seaweed commodities constitute a global challenge that demands
a coordinated, cross-sectoral response. A compilation of disease reports in cultivated sea-
weeds up to 2025 (Table 1) indicates diverse outbreaks across major producer nations. The
Aquaculture International (2026) 34:97                                        Page 35 of 53 97


magnitude of the economic losses and social impacts, particularly for millions of coastal
farmers, underscores that disease management cannot rest with any single actor. Multisec-
tor collaboration involving government, the scientific community, industry, local commu-
nities, and international organisations is required to strengthen prevention, early detection,
reporting, and control throughout the seaweed supply chain (Jompa et al. 2024). From a
scientific standpoint, strengthening the foundations of seaweed pathology is central to col-
laboration. Genomic and metagenomic surveillance at farm sites, integrated with system-
atic recording of symptom phenotypes, severity, and transmissibility, accelerates pathogen
identification; meetings and workshops help standardise protocols, share resources, and
align analytics, thereby improving diagnostics, guiding evidence-based control strategies,
and deepening understanding of interactions between hosts and pathogens. From the per-
spective of policy and governance, interdisciplinary approaches are needed to design man-
agement strategies, farming practices, and regulatory frameworks that minimise disease
risk while supporting the sustainable development goals, especially SDGs 14 on life below
water (Spillias et al. 2022; Vadassery and Neish 2024). Empirically grounded social-eco-
logical frameworks offer a lens for assessing trade-offs and co-benefits in decision making
across actors (Cerca et al. 2023). At the implementation level, participatory methods such
as group model building facilitate stakeholder engagement, surface critical sustainability
issues, and map leverage points for intervention, including those related to disease impacts
in seaweed aquaculture systems (Muflikh et al. 2024).
   Operationally, multisector collaboration should be translated into clear workflow mech-
anisms. First, two-way reporting channels at the farmer level should be strengthened for
any anomalies indicating emerging disease. Second, rapid scientific responses are needed
through aetiological investigation, risk assessment, and evidence-based recommendations.
Third, relevant authorities should formulate adaptive policies for prevention, control, and
impact mitigation, including guidance on good aquaculture practices, biosecurity, and post-
harvest management. Consistent feedback loops between the field, laboratory, and regula-
tors ensure continuous learning and progressively more precise responses over time. Data
infrastructure is a critical node connecting all these elements. Standardised documentation
of pathogen diversity, biogeography, host range, outbreak dynamics, and cross-sector meta-
data should be developed into a globally accessible database via a web portal to expedite
detection, enhance cross-study comparability, and strengthen response planning (Stritt-
matter et al. 2022). Applying principles of data openness and interoperability will bolster
transparency, replication, and knowledge transfer across regions (Mwanyoka et al. 2025).
To accelerate collaboration and capacity, institutional platforms play a strategic role. For
example, the Government of Indonesia’s initiative to establish the international tropical
seaweed research centre (ITSRC) could serve as a hub for research, technology transfer,
and capacity development. Through a coordinated multi-stakeholder network, the ITSRC
has the potential to foster a resilient research ecosystem capable of addressing disease
challenges while promoting sustainable growth of the seaweed industry. Taken together,
integrating scientific, policy, operational, data, and institutional dimensions within a single
multisector collaborative framework offers a realistic and effective pathway to reduce the
disease burden in seaweed aquaculture.


Application of pathogen and disease detection methods

By 2025, the integration of cutting-edge technologies, rigorous research methodologies,
and cross-national collaboration produced increasingly sensitive and accurate tools to
97 Page 36 of 53                                             Aquaculture International (2026) 34:97


identify pathogens in farmed seaweeds. Applying these methods has enhanced biosecurity
and production stability through early detection, which is crucial for preventing economic
losses. Despite this progress, knowledge gaps concerning viruses, particularly their ecol-
ogy and diversity, remain evident. Rather than a reason to delay intervention, these limi-
tations underscore the urgency of further research to strengthen detection and mitigation
strategies. Pioneering studies have begun to address this gap. Small interfering RNA-based
approaches have been used to detect novel RNA and DNA viruses (Dekker et al. 2024),
while virome metagenomic sequencing has been applied to characterise viral communities,
including those associated with BD in Ulva (van der Loos et al. 2023). Given that this topic
was relatively underexplored prior to 2020, these findings represent important milestones,
although causal links between viruses and clinical symptoms still require experimental
validation.
    In Asia, research and field monitoring have focused more on bacterial and parasitic path-
ogens. Histology, with and without staining, has been employed to assess pathogen impacts
at the tissue level (Riyaz et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2021). However, reliance on morphologi-
cal change makes early detection challenging, since lesions often become apparent only at
advanced stages. Accordingly, integrating higher resolution microscopy such as SEM and
TEM with molecular analyses and other omics such as metabolomics has been proposed to
deepen understanding of seaweed pathology and accelerate diagnosis (Kwak et al. 2017;
Ward et al. 2020; Kim et al. 2014). At the molecular level, several breakthroughs have
improved diagnostic accuracy and speed. Quantitative PCR or qPCR enables high preci-
sion quantification of pathogens (Ferrari et al. 2021; Liu et al. 2024a) and PCR restriction
fragment length polymorphism or PCR RFLP based on the mitochondrial cytochrome c
oxidase subunit 2 cox2 has been reported as effective for detecting RRD in Pyropia (Lee
and Lee 2022). Previously, highly specific cox1 and cox2 markers for Pythium were devel-
oped for long-term monitoring of infection and distribution patterns of individual Pythium
in Pyropia aquaculture ponds, thereby providing information to predict infection and pre-
vent mass mortality due to RRD (Lee et al. 2017; Qiu et al. 2019). In addition, nanotech-
nology likewise offers opportunities for detection at very low concentrations using devices
that are cost efficient and easy to operate (Khan et al. 2023). Gold nanoparticle based nano-
biosensors provide rapid tests through changes in plasmonic properties that indicate the
presence of viral particles. This approach has been successfully applied to viruses of land
crops and is expected to be adaptable to seaweed viruses (Kulabhusan et al. 2022; Gurung
et al. 2023).
    Artificial intelligence is expanding the horizon for real-time monitoring of seaweed
health. Computer-vision systems based on machine-learning approaches, including a
robotic platform employing the k-nearest neighbours algorithm in Indonesia, have dem-
onstrated the ability to identify IID from thallus colour and texture features with a field
accuracy of 86.7% (Saputro et al. 2024). This approach enhances detection efficiency
while reducing reliance on manual inspection, making it particularly relevant for medium
to large-scale farming operations. Integration with the Internet of Things (IoT), through
underwater cameras, unmanned vehicles, and environmental sensors, further enables the
continuous collection of temperature, salinity, nutrient, and biomass data. Such systems
support early detection of physiological stress or infection and facilitate data-driven deci-
sion-making. In parallel, optical remote-sensing technologies are emerging as key com-
ponents of early disease detection. Hyper- and multispectral sensors allow the measure-
ment of thallus reflectance across multiple wavelengths, enabling infection-related colour
changes to be detected before they become visually apparent.
Aquaculture International (2026) 34:97                                       Page 37 of 53 97


    For example, Alevizos et al. (2024) utilised drone-based reflectance data and vegetation
indices combined with machine-learning algorithms to distinguish healthy and IID-infected
K. alvarezii and E. denticulatum, achieving classification accuracies ranging from 83 to
99% based on spectral signatures and selected indices. Although the spatial resolution of
satellite imagery remains insufficient for mapping individual seaweed farms (Kellaris et al.
2019), remote sensing nonetheless holds promise as an early-warning system for large-
scale or extraordinary events, such as regional mass bleaching episodes. Moreover, low-
cost underwater stereo-camera systems integrated with computer-vision techniques have
successfully been applied to monitor Saccharina growth in Europe, and similar approaches
could be adapted to automate the detection of disease symptoms, including necrotic lesions
or epiphyte overgrowth, using machine-learning frameworks (Gerlo et al. 2023).
    Looking ahead, mainstreaming these technologies into integrated disease management
should be a priority agenda. Strategies need to combine preventive, curative, and predictive
approaches tailored to local farming environments. Successful implementation depends on
standardisation of terminology and protocols, cost reductions to ensure affordability for
smallholders, and improved usability in remote settings with limited infrastructure (Cot-
tier-Cook et al. 2021; Strittmatter et al. 2022; Murúa et al. 2023). Overall, by 2025, there
is a clear shift toward proactive, sensor and data-driven seaweed health management. With
continued refinement spanning molecular methods such as PCR, qPCR, and LAMP, nano-
biosensors, artificial intelligence connected to the IoT, and optical imaging, the seaweed
industry is poised to reduce outbreak risks, increase yields, and safeguard the livelihoods
of millions of coastal farmers (Cottier-Cook et al. 2021; Alevizos et al. 2024). Challenges
in standardisation, cost, and device robustness remain to be addressed, yet the current tra-
jectory suggests the sector is ready for broad adoption.



Conclusion and future outlook

As of 2025, global seaweed production has experienced disease outbreaks of increasing
frequency and severity, with at least 16 distinct diseases identified (see Table 1). In tropi-
cal waters, IID remains the most frequently reported disease affecting cultivated seaweeds.
In contrast, in temperate to cold-water regions, the disease profile is more diverse, with
multiple forms of rot and spot diseases representing a major source of diseases and posing
challenges to the productivity of seaweed aquaculture in these geographical areas (Fig. 1).
Outbreaks tend to spread rapidly and are often triggered by environmental stressors (see
Fig. 3). The open-sea farming context limits control over environmental variables. Even so,
the past years have brought substantial advances in understanding aetiology, transmission
dynamics, and mitigation options. Persistent barriers arise from gaps in official reporting
and the absence of international standards for outbreak identification and response. At the
operational level, farmers often rely on unvalidated empirical actions, including the dis-
posal of infected seaweeds or the use of inorganic inputs. Such measures may yield short-
term benefits yet risk long-term ecological impacts.
   Amid accelerating climate change, the need to strengthen biosecurity is increasingly
urgent. Reports of losses that can reach 100% of production when outbreaks go unchecked
underscore the scale of risk to farmers and supply chains (Suyo-Diala and Hurtado 2024).
Evidence of pathogen invasion in wild red seaweeds in Chile highlights the importance of
systemic surveillance and risk-based interventions across the aquaculture value chain, from
seedstock to product distribution (Murúa et al. 2024). Multi-stakeholder collaboration is
97 Page 38 of 53                                                     Aquaculture International (2026) 34:97


essential, an exemplary case of such collaboration is the Centre of Excellence for Seaweed
(PUIRL, formerly Centre of Excellence for Development and Utilisation of Seaweeds) at
Hasanuddin University, Indonesia, which brought together academics, government entities,
and farmers in the implementation of innovative seaweed farming practices (Kasmiati et al.
2024). The complexity of disease biology, which often exceeds expectations, demands
continuous innovation and research designs that respond to day-to-day farming realities,
including local environmental variability and the dynamic behaviour of pathogens (Faisan
et al. 2021).
    Molecular markers are beginning to support the selection of disease-resistant lines
without eroding germplasm diversity, enabling breeding programmes to proceed in a
more targeted way while safeguarding the diversity needed for long-term resilience
(Tan et al. 2024). Moreover, modern technologies in monitoring seaweed health, such
as remote sensing, are becoming increasingly crucial for early detection of potential dis-
ease outbreaks. Proximal optical sensing enables the identification of symptoms with
high accuracy, allowing interventions before declines in farm performance become vis-
ible (Alevizos et al. 2024). With ongoing development and evaluation, these innovations
are anticipated to play a pivotal role in addressing disease challenges and promoting the
establishment of sustainable aquaculture in the future.
    The future research and policy agenda should align several complementary priori-
ties within a coherent workstream. Standardisation of terminology, diagnostic crite-
ria, and reporting protocols needs to be accelerated so that data across locations can
be compared and acted upon without delay. Risk-based biosecurity must be reinforced
from seedstock through post-harvest via quarantine, health certification, and sustained
environmental monitoring. Diagnostic technologies that are accessible to small-scale
farmers should be scaled, including portable molecular platforms, optical sensing, and
early warning systems that use environmental data. Breeding and selection programmes
should deliver lines with stable resistance across environmental conditions while main-
taining genetic diversity. Collaborative governance that bridges laboratories, farmers,
government, and international networks should be strengthened through participatory
mechanisms such as My Seaweed Looks Weird (MSLW) web portal to accelerate com-
munity reporting and response, together with enhanced local diagnostic capacity and
transparent cross-border reporting to speed collective learning and reduce transbound-
ary risk (Strittmatter et al. 2022).
    With a combination of intensive research, context-appropriate technological innova-
tion, and consistent policy support, the seaweed industry can grow in a sustainable, pro-
ductive, and resilient manner. Illustrative implementation that balances economic objec-
tives, social well-being, and environmental integrity is emerging in K. alvarezii farming
in India, which demonstrates an integrated approach to productivity gains and system
resilience (Mantri et al. 2024). Ultimately, success in controlling seaweed diseases is
determined not only by the sophistication of technology but also by the quality of col-
laboration, the consistency of policy, and the commitment of an innovation ecosystem
oriented toward sustainability.
