ISF "Unlocking Indonesia's Blue Economy through Financing, Industry-Led Innovation and Scalable Partnerships
blue economy = ekonomi yang utilize sumber daya laut
Indonesia produces huge amounts of seaweed and fish but exports most of it raw and captures little value. The panel looked at what it takes to turn pilots into a real industry: industry needs steady, quality supply and guaranteed buyers; government is building model sites, training farmers and offering cheap loans; and banks say capital exists, but most blue economy businesses aren't "bankable" yet, partly because the DATA investors need doesn't exist. The moderator's closing definition of a scalable model: ecologically sustainable, financially investible, inclusive, MEASURABLE, and replicable.

INDUSTRY: THE PROBLEM IS VALUE, NOT VOLUME (APINDO)
Value capture problem
Indonesia produces 10M+ tons of seaweed per year, but most is exported RAW. Other countries process it and keep the profit.
Processing it domestically could raise its value 6–18x, depending on the product.
Goal: move from "raw material supplier" to "seaweed processing industry."
Chicken-and-egg problem
Farmers won't invest in quality unless buyers pay a premium.
Processors won't build factories unless supply is steady and reliable.
Neither exists yet, so each side waits for the other.
Jobs for coastal youth
Build processing facilities NEAR the farms, so young people don't have to migrate to cities for work. => giving jobs to those who actually live there.
APINDO's initiative: "Indonesian Seaweed Incorporation"
Uses APINDO's corporate members as potential BUYERS (off-takers) and connects the chain: farmers → processors → corporate buyers.
Product focus: seaweed BIOSTIMULANT, a natural seaweed-based product that helps crops grow and cope with stress. It's relatively low-tech, so it suits local facilities. It adds to existing carrageenan processing (carrageenan = seaweed thickener used in food and cosmetics).
Global biostimulant market: ~USD 1.9 billion. Indonesia's share: under 1%.
The pilot (supported by Standard Chartered and partners)
Track 1: responsible farming and good-quality seedlings.
Track 2: demo plots applying the biostimulant on several crops to PROVE it works.
Universities and independent parties verify the results.
First results expected early 2027, then a full "island model" (one complete working supply chain that can be copied elsewhere).
What's needed to scale nationally
MARKET: companies that host trials and then commit to buying.
EVIDENCE: independent, university-verified data from MULTIPLE regions.
REGULATION: a national product standard and registration for seaweed biostimulants, so buyers have a quality benchmark.
SUPPLY ("supply, supply, supply"): farmer education, standard practices, good seedlings, more farmers.

PART B – GOVERNMENT: WHAT KKP IS DOING (DG of Aquaculture)
Context: KKP's Blue Economy policy has five pillars, from ocean health to plastic waste. Sustainable aquaculture is one of them and a direct presidential mandate.
KKP is focusing on 5 priority aquaculture commodities: shrimp, tilapia, mud crab, SEAWEED and lobster. They chose these because the farming technology is already mastered and the market opportunity is strong.
How the government supports them (5 actions):
REGULATION – e.g., a new 2026 ministerial regulation (Permen KP No. 5/2026 (?)) supporting sustainable lobster aquaculture.
MODELING SITES – one demonstration farm per commodity, used as "centers of excellence" where investors and stakeholders can come and see best practice:
Shrimp: Kebumen, Central Java
Tilapia: Karawang, West Java
Mud crab: location unclear in transcript (?)
SEAWEED: Wakatobi (?)
Lobster: Batam → This is effectively his answer to "if an investor says yes tomorrow, where do I send them first?": go to these modeling sites.
REVITALIZING OLD PONDS on Java's north coast (Pantura), starting in West Java with 2 of 4 planned regencies (likely Bekasi and Subang (?)). This means better infrastructure and facilities, higher productivity, and best-practice farming.
EXTENSION OFFICERS – about 3,000–4,000 field officers who guide and assist farmers daily, especially micro and small businesses.
ACCESS TO FINANCE – through a KKP public service agency (BLU LPMUKP, the Marine and Fisheries Business Capital Management Agency) that lends to micro and small farmers at competitive (low) interest rates.

PART C – SCALABLE PARTNERSHIP: BABCOCK'S MARITIME PARTNERSHIP PROGRAMME
Babcock is a UK defense and engineering company with a maritime footprint in Indonesia, Australia and the UK.
The Maritime Partnership Programme (MPP) was signed by President Prabowo and the UK Prime Minister in January 2026 and is backed by about GBP 4 billion from UK Export Finance (the UK government's export credit agency). Babcock is the lead partner delivering it.
Main focus: FOOD SECURITY (a Prabowo priority) through MODERNIZING FISHING VESSELS.
Claimed benefits: • More and better fish for domestic nutrition, plus export potential • Better living conditions for fishermen on board (kitchens, rooms, toilets) • Longer-lasting vessels, so less waste • Shipyard and industrial jobs • Technology and knowledge transfer: scholarships, university R&D consortiums, partnerships with local shipbuilders along the supply chain
Note: this is about capture fisheries (fishing boats), NOT aquaculture or seaweed.

PART D – FINANCE: WHY "CAPITAL IS AVAILABLE BUT NOT READY" (Standard Chartered)
The size of the opportunity
A Standard Chartered report estimates the ocean economy could need/attract up to USD 2.5 trillion in investment across the region over the next decade: ~USD 1 trillion public, ~USD 1.5 trillion private.
The problem is diversity and readiness, not money
Indonesia alone has 17,000+ islands. Projects range from early ideas to mature businesses, and each stage has a different risk profile.
"There's a lot of capital out there. But is it the RIGHT type of capital, with the right risk appetite? Are projects bankable?"
Three types of finance, matched to stage
Idea stage → project preparation / development capital (grants, technical support to get started)
Emerging solution → venture capital, sometimes private equity
Proven, scaling → debt: project finance, blue bonds/blue finance, etc. ("Bankable" = safe and profitable enough that a bank will lend to it.)
The biggest mismatch is DATA (?)
He said there's a mismatch between investors, project developers and people on the ground, and that one of the biggest gaps is data. The transcript is garbled here, but that's the most likely reading.
What Standard Chartered is doing
Involvement with the Earthshot Prize (Prince William's environmental prize) to find solutions.
Launched a program through its FOUNDATION (not commercial banking money) last year, focused on Indonesia: • Project preparation and capacity building • Working with government and corporates to "aggregate" small enterprises (group them so they're big enough to finance) • Goal: move enterprises from early stage → investible → then hand them to commercial banking to scale across markets.
⚠️ Important: the moderator asked "what SPECIFIC METRICS make an enterprise financeable for a commercial bank?" He did NOT give specific metrics. That question remains unanswered.

RELEVANCE TO OUR NASA PROJECT (team note – seaweed farm focus)
Strongest hooks:
SUPPLY RELIABILITY – APINDO's #1 scaling condition. Buyers and processors need to trust that quality seaweed keeps arriving. Satellite data (sea surface temperature, chlorophyll, rainfall/salinity proxies) could flag heat stress or disease-prone conditions (e.g., ice-ice) before crops fail.
DATA FOR BANKABILITY – Standard Chartered named data as a key investor–developer mismatch, and the moderator listed MEASURABLE as a scaling criterion. Independent, consistent, multi-region environmental data is exactly what satellites provide.
REAL USERS OUTSIDE GOVERNMENT – APINDO's Seaweed Incorporation and its off-takers (sourcing decisions), farmers (planting/harvest timing), and potentially lenders (risk assessment).
A REAL TEST SITE – KKP's seaweed modeling site (Wakatobi (?)) is a possible place to validate our tool against ground truth.
Be careful in our pitch:
Nobody on the panel asked for satellite monitoring. The link is our inference.
Their biggest stated gaps (product standards, off-take contracts, processing plants) are things our tool cannot fix.
Most seaweed lines are smaller than one satellite pixel, so we'd be reading bay-level conditions, not individual farms.
"What metrics make an enterprise bankable" was asked and NOT answered. Don't claim the bank wants our data specifically; ask them.
